## Before we begin

Tonight's story comes from the very edge of the Roman Empire — a fort called Vindolanda (vin-doh-LAN-dah) in the windy north of the country we now call England, about nineteen hundred years ago. Rome's soldiers here wrote letters on thin slivers of wood, in ink, and threw them away when done — into mud that kept them safe for us to find. Two names to know, both real: Sulpicia Lepidina (sul-PIK-ee-ah leh-pih-DEE-nah), wife of the fort's commander; and her dear friend Claudia Severa (SAY-vair-ah), who lives at another fort a day's ride away. The post is about to arrive.

## The story

*Claudia Severa to her Lepidina, greetings.*

On the third day before the Ides of September, sister, for the day of the celebration of my birthday, I give you a warm invitation to make sure that you come to us, to make the day more enjoyable for me by your arrival...

Give my greetings to your Cerialis. My Aelius and my little son send you their greetings.

And then, because some things cannot be trusted to a secretary, Severa took the pen herself and added, in her own quick hand:

*I shall expect you, sister. Farewell, sister, my dearest soul, as I hope to prosper, and hail.*

---

At Vindolanda, Lepidina read the letter twice, smiling, and then looked out at the weather, which was doing what the weather in north Britain always did.

"Marcus!" she called. The clerk appeared, ink-stained, trailed by the smell of the hundred fires of the fort — bread, smoke, wet wool, leather. "A reply to the lady Severa. And letters to go south with the next post rider."

Marcus readied his thin wooden leaves — smooth slivers of birch, thin as a knife blade, that folded shut like little books. Everyone at the fort wrote constantly: requests, reports, and above all lists. The empire, Marcus liked to say, was actually made of lists, with some soldiers attached.

"Ready, my lady."

"To Severa: *I will come.* Say it prettily — you know the forms. Say the commander's business permitting, but say I will come regardless of the commander's business, and phrase it so my husband can laugh but not object."

"The exact phrasing of that, my lady, is above my pay."

"Then earn a raise. Next: to my husband's supplier at Coria. We need pepper if there's any to be had, and more of those chickens. And to my sister-in-law: thank her for the socks. Say the socks are the talk of the fort. Do not say that this is because winter here frightens Italians into poetry — write that part, actually. She'll enjoy it."

Marcus wrote. Outside, boots tramped: the garrison — big cheerful Batavians from the flat river-country across the sea, auxiliary soldiers earning their Roman citizenship the long way — was changing watch. From the commander's quarters came the shrieks of children playing chase; from somewhere beyond, a soldier's voice complaining, with feeling, that the beer had run out *again* — someone really ought to write headquarters.

"Someone did," said Marcus, without looking up. "Masclus, of the first cohort. 'My fellow soldiers have no beer, which we ask to be sent.' A model of military correspondence. Clear need, brisk request, only slightly desperate."

"And headquarters replied?"

"Beer is coming up the road, they say. So is winter. It will be a race."

Lepidina laughed and sealed her letters. She thought — as she often thought — how strange and how ordinary her life was. Here they sat at the world's end, a wooden fort in a green wilderness the maps barely knew, garrisoned by Dutchmen, supplied with pepper from India if the roads were kind. Her friend lived one fort along an empire that stretched from here to the deserts of Arabia — and what did they write to each other across that unimaginable distance? *Come to my birthday. Bring the children. I miss you.*

"Marcus. When you file the copies — what happens to all our letters, in the end? A hundred years from now, say."

Marcus considered the ink on his fingers. "Honestly, my lady? Rubbish heaps. We throw them out by the basket. The damp takes them. Nothing so thoroughly lost as an old letter."

"Hm. A pity," said Lepidina. "Some of them are rather nice."

---

Marcus was wrong, though it took nineteen centuries to prove it.

The wet ground under Vindolanda — airless, black, peaty — kept the thrown-away letters like a strongbox. In 1973, archaeologists digging in the buried layers began to pull out slivers of wood with faint ink writing: the beer complaint; the socks and underpants; the lists; the children's writing exercises; and one letter in two hands, inviting Lepidina to a birthday party on the third day before the Ides of September.

Severa's postscript — *Farewell, sister, my dearest soul* — is the earliest known Latin written by a woman's own hand. Her birthday is the first birthday party in Britain we can put a date to. And on the shelves of a great museum, her invitation lies in a glass case, still saying, across all that time, the only thing it ever said: *come, sister. Make my day happier. I shall expect you.*

There is no record of the party. But she went. On a green frontier at the world's end, with the beer wagon racing the winter — of course she went.

## What's true in this story

- Claudia Severa, Sulpicia Lepidina, their husbands Aelius Brocchus and Flavius Cerialis, and the birthday invitation are all real. The letter — Vindolanda Tablet 291, written around 100 CE — says very nearly what the story quotes, including the date (three days before the Ides of September, i.e. 11 September) and the affectionate postscript in Severa's own handwriting.
- That postscript really is the earliest surviving Latin known to be written by a woman's hand, and the invitation is often called Britain's oldest birthday-party record. Marcus the clerk is invented; the commander's household, with wives and children at the fort (children's shoes have been dug up), is not.
- The writing tablets are real: leaf-thin slivers of wood, written in ink and folded shut, preserved by oxygen-free waterlogged soil and rediscovered from 1973 onward. Hundreds have been read, and they're mostly gloriously ordinary — lists, requests, complaints, gossip.
- The beer letter is genuine: the cavalry officer Masclus really wrote that his fellow soldiers had no beer (*cervesa*) and asked for more to be sent. The letter mentioning warm socks and underpants sent to a soldier is Tablet 346 — northern Britain's weather has always demanded tribute.
- The garrison really was Batavian and Tungrian auxiliaries from the Low Countries (roughly the modern Netherlands and Belgium) — non-citizens serving for pay and eventual citizenship. One tablet calls the locals "Brittunculi," "wretched little Britons," the empire being no kinder than empires are.
- Vindolanda in this period was a timber fort on the northern frontier — Hadrian's Wall itself wouldn't be begun until the 120s, a generation after our letter. Dates around the tablets are approximate (roughly 85–105 CE for this period of the fort), so the story says "about."
- Whether Lepidina attended the party, no tablet says. The story's last line is hope, not evidence — but it's how everyone who reads the invitation feels.
