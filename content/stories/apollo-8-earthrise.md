## Before we begin

Tonight's story happens in December 1968 — before phones, before laptops, when a computer filled a room — and it happens in two places at once: in Houston, Texas, at NASA's mission control, and two hundred and forty thousand miles away, around the Moon. Three astronauts to meet: Frank Borman (BOR-man), the commander; Jim Lovell (LUV-ul); and Bill Anders (AN-ders). No one has ever left Earth's orbit before. Their ship is Apollo 8 (uh-POL-oh). Much of what you'll hear tonight are their real recorded words. Headsets on.

## The story

Susan Borman had privately decided her husband had a fifty-fifty chance of coming home, and had also decided to say nothing of the kind out loud, and both decisions were still holding on the morning of December 21st, 1968, when the biggest rocket ever built lifted her husband off the planet.

It had all happened so fast. The Moon ship wasn't ready; the year was a wound — assassinations, riots, a bitter war on the news each night — and in the middle of it, NASA made the boldest call of the age: send Apollo 8 around the Moon itself. No landing. Just the going — farther than any human had ever gone, on a rocket that had carried men exactly zero times before.

Three days the families listened to the little squawk boxes NASA put in their homes, the crackle of their husbands and fathers falling *up*, outward, while the Earth shrank behind them. And on the morning of Christmas Eve, the ship swung behind the Moon — where no radio can reach — and everyone on Earth held one long breath until the signal came back exactly on time, and a human voice described the Moon from up close for the first time in the history of the species.

It was, the astronauts agreed, magnificent and forbidding — gray as beach sand and pounded everywhere with craters. No color. No sound. No life. They were scientists on a survey, and they photographed it dutifully, orbit after orbit, cameras aimed down at the dead ground.

Then, on the fourth orbit, the ship happened to roll — and something came up over the Moon's horizon that was not on the flight plan.

"Oh my God! Look at that picture over there!" — that's Bill Anders, on the cockpit tape, Christmas Eve, 1968. "There's the Earth coming up. Wow, is that pretty!"

Blue. That was the thing. A quarter of a million miles of blackness, a dead gray world below — and rising over it, small enough to hide behind a thumb, the only colored thing in the universe: blue oceans, white weather, home. Everyone alive, everyone who had ever lived — from up there it all fit behind a thumb.

"Hey, don't take that," Borman deadpanned — commander's humor — "it's not scheduled."

Anders, laughing, already shooting in black and white, called for what the moment deserved: "You got a color film, Jim? Hand me that roll of color, quick, would you—"

And so the first Earthrise ever witnessed dissolved into three humans scrambling around a cabin the size of a car, hunting film. Lovell dug for the color magazine — seconds ticking, the Earth climbing — the view slid out of one window, someone found it in another, and Anders got it: *click.* One frame of a half-lit blue marble over a gray horizon. They didn't know it yet, but they had just taken one of the most famous photographs that will ever exist.

That evening — Christmas Eve — the three of them made a television broadcast to the planet they could hide behind a thumb. More people watched and listened, it's estimated, than had ever watched anything at once before. NASA had told them only: say something appropriate. Say something appropriate, to everyone on Earth, on Christmas Eve, from the Moon.

So as the gray craters rolled beneath them, they took turns reading the opening of the oldest story they knew: *In the beginning...* — Anders, then Lovell, then Borman, the words older than every nation listening, read over the sound of static from another world. And Borman closed it:

"And from the crew of Apollo 8, we close with good night, good luck, a Merry Christmas — and God bless all of you, all of you on the good Earth."

In living rooms everywhere — in Houston where the flight controllers had stopped, headsets half off; in three particular houses where three particular families sat very close to the radio — a whole battered year went quiet for a minute.

There was one more long breath to hold. Early Christmas morning, behind the Moon, out of contact, the crew had to fire the engine that would bring them home. If it failed, nothing on Earth could help them. Around the world, mission control's clocks counted down to the moment the ship would appear — if the burn had worked. The static broke right on time, and Jim Lovell's voice came across a quarter million miles:

"Please be informed: there is a Santa Claus."

They splashed down in the dark Pacific two days later, frogmen and helicopters and home by New Year's, and Susan Borman revised her arithmetic with pleasure. Among the thousands of telegrams that poured in, Frank Borman kept one, from a stranger, all his life. It said: *Thank you, Apollo 8. You saved 1968.*

And the photograph? They had flown a quarter of a million miles to study the Moon — and the picture that changed the world was the one they took looking back. *Earthrise* went onto front pages, posters, a postage stamp; within sixteen months, millions marked the first Earth Day, and to this day people say that photo taught humanity what "home" means: one small blue boat of a planet, everyone aboard together.

Borman, Lovell, and Anders went to the Moon and discovered the Earth. It's up there in your sky tonight — the Moon they circled — and here you are on the good Earth, tucked in safe.

Good night, good luck, and God bless all of you.

## What's true in this story

- Apollo 8 launched on 21 December 1968 with Frank Borman, Jim Lovell, and Bill Anders — the first humans to leave Earth orbit, the first to ride the Saturn V, and the first to orbit the Moon: ten orbits over Christmas Eve and Christmas Day. The bold decision to go had been made only that summer, with the lunar module far behind schedule and the space race pressing.
- The Earthrise dialogue is from the actual onboard tape, lightly trimmed: Anders' "Oh my God! Look at that picture over there!...Wow, is that pretty!", Borman's joke about it not being scheduled, and the scramble for color film are all real. NASA's frame-by-frame reconstruction confirmed Anders took the famous color photograph on 24 December, during the fourth orbit, after the spacecraft's roll happened to bring Earth into view.
- The Christmas Eve broadcast happened as told: the crew read Genesis in turns, and Borman's sign-off — "good night, good luck, a Merry Christmas, and God bless all of you, all of you on the good Earth" — is verbatim. It's often described as the most-watched broadcast up to that time; the "one billion" figures are estimates, so the story says "it's estimated."
- "Please be informed: there is a Santa Claus" is Lovell's real transmission after the engine burn behind the Moon on Christmas morning committed them to the trip home. Splashdown came 27 December in the Pacific.
- The telegram Borman treasured — "Thank you, Apollo 8. You saved 1968" — is real, from a member of the public, after a year of assassinations (Martin Luther King Jr., Robert Kennedy), riots, and war.
- Susan Borman's private fifty-fifty estimate is her own later account. The families really did listen at home on NASA "squawk boxes."
- *Earthrise* is widely credited with helping ignite the environmental movement — the first Earth Day followed in April 1970 — and has been called the most influential environmental photograph ever taken. And no, Apollo 8 never landed; that was Apollo 11, seven months later — walking on the Moon partly because these three proved you could get there.
