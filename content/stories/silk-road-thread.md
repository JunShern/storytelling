## Before we begin

Tonight's story begins in China more than two thousand years ago, in the great capital Chang'an (chahng-AHN), and ends in Rome — though nothing in the story travels that whole way except the storyteller itself: a bolt of silk. Silk is cloth spun from the cocoons of silkworms, and how it was made was China's most closely guarded secret. Two more words: a *caravan* (KA-ra-van) is a company of traders and pack animals crossing the desert together; and an *oasis* (oh-AY-siss) is a green watering place in the desert where towns grow. Now hush — the cloth is speaking.

## The story

I began as breakfast. Be patient — it's a good beginning.

In a village of mulberry trees, a girl named Little Wren fed leaves to ten thousand silkworms in trays, and complained about it to each one personally. "Eat, eat, eat. Your whole job is eating." All summer the worms munched — a sound like soft rain — until each spun itself a white cocoon from a single thread, wound round and round, longer than three hundred houses set end to end.

Wren's grandmother unwound me from my cocoons — several threads twisted together for strength — and wove me on the clacking loom, and there I first became *me*: a bolt of silk, smooth as poured water, dyed the color of dawn. Wren pressed her cheek against me. "This one's too beautiful to sell."

"That's the beautiful ones' job," said Grandmother. "Off it goes to pay our taxes. Cloth is money, child — the empire itself counts its treasure in bolts of silk."

So I went to Chang'an in a tax cart, and I will boast now: the capital was the grandest city under heaven, walls within walls, markets roaring in a dozen languages. There a trader bought me — Master Gao, who smelled of tea and long roads — and rolled me tight, and spoke to me the way travelers speak to their goods. "Well, Dawn-cloth. Westward, then. Do you know how the west road was opened? A court traveler named Zhang Qian went out a hundred years ago to find allies — was captured, kept ten years, escaped, and walked home with something better than an ally: *knowledge*. Roads. Kingdoms nobody had names for. Horses that sweat like heroes. The Son of Heaven has wanted the west ever since. And the west, little cloth, wants *you*."

What came next, no one had warned me about: the desert. Days like hammered brass, nights cold as a knife's flat, and under me the swaying, grumbling, magnificent engine of it all — a two-humped camel, who could drink a pond on Monday and stride to Friday, carrying half a market on her back. At every oasis town — mud walls, sudden green, the smell of melons and dust — I was unrolled, admired, haggled over.

And here is the thing you must understand about my road, the thing the maps never show: *nobody walks it end to end.* Not Master Gao, not anyone. At Dunhuang, Gao sold me to a Sogdian dealer with rings on every finger. Beyond the mountains, the Sogdian sold me to a Persian. The Persian's cousin carried me through Parthia, where the tolls have tolls; a Greek bought me by a blue harbor. I crossed the whole world the way a bucket crosses a fire line — hand to hand to hand — and at every hand my price climbed another rung, until I heard myself spoken of in whispers, like a jewel with manners.

Somewhere in Parthia I overheard the truth about my secret. A Roman merchant asked my Persian keeper: "What *is* silk? They say in Rome it grows on trees in the land of the Seres — combed from the leaves like wool."

The Persian smiled like a locked box. "Certainly. Trees. Combed at dawn," he said, and winked at me, and I kept my silence, thinking of Wren and ten thousand chewing worms. Let Rome believe in wool-trees. The middlemen fatten on the mystery, and China keeps its worms; anyone who smuggles the secret out answers for it with his life — or so the traders mutter.

Rome, when I finally rolled into it after a year and more on the road — Rome was mad for me. I'm told the wise old men of the Senate grumbled that silk was ruining the republic's purses and blushes both, that one crabby scholar computed rivers of gold flowing east each year for luxuries, sighing that we cost the empire a fortune so its ladies could "shimmer in public." I heard it from inside the sedan chair of the senator's wife who wore me, so their protest seemed to me — how shall I put it — incompletely successful.

She wore me for years. I was mended, treasured, handed down. And once, at a dinner, a geographer held my hem to the lamp and said: "Consider — this cloth has seen the whole earth. The Seres who made it imagine *us* as a mirror of themselves, a great orderly kingdom at the world's other rim. Two empires, dreaming of each other, and only the silk travels between."

He was almost right. But lying warm across a sleeping shoulder that night, I thought of what I'd really seen, which no emperor of either end ever saw: the whole road at once. The worms' soft rain. Wren's cheek. The camel's patient heart. A hundred hands, brown and pale, calloused and ringed, passing me along like a promise nobody agreed to but everybody kept.

Empires dream of each other. But it's the hands that hold the world together — one bolt of cloth at a time.

Sleep now. I'll keep the shoulder warm.

## What's true in this story

- Wren, Gao, and the silk's itinerary are invented, but every step is historical. Silk really is reeled from silkworm cocoons — each an unbroken filament hundreds of meters long — from worms fed on mulberry leaves; China guarded the method for centuries, and ancient sources report the secret was protected on pain of death (how strictly, we can't verify — the story hedges it into a mutter).
- Bolts of silk genuinely served as money in Han China — taxes and salaries were paid in cloth and grain.
- Zhang Qian's journey is real: sent west by the Han emperor around 138 BCE, captured and held about ten years, he returned with the first solid knowledge of Central Asia — the trip that effectively opened the Silk Road. The "heavenly horses" of Ferghana were a real obsession of the Han court.
- The relay is the truest thing in the story: virtually no one traveled the road end to end. Goods passed through many hands — Chinese, Sogdian, Persian, Greek, and more — with the price rising at each exchange, and Parthian middlemen profitably in the way. When a Chinese envoy, Gan Ying, tried to reach Rome around 97 CE, Parthian sailors talked him out of it.
- Rome's silk fever is documented: the Senate under Tiberius restricted silk-wearing by men; Pliny the Elder groused about the empire's gold draining east for luxuries (his figure of 100 million sesterces is rhetoric, so the silk calls it a crabby scholar's computation); and Seneca sniffed that silk left women "clothed, yet naked."
- Each end of the road misimagined the other, just as the geographer says: Romans called the Chinese the "Seres" — silk-people — and some believed silk was combed from trees; Chinese writers pictured Rome ("Daqin") as a kind of mirror-China. Two-humped Bactrian camels, oasis towns like Dunhuang, and year-plus travel times are all accurate.
- One anachronism to confess: nobody in the story would have said "Silk Road." The name was invented by a German geographer in 1877, two thousand years after our cloth made the trip.
