## Before we begin

Tonight's story takes place in Baghdad, in the country we now call Iraq — almost twelve hundred years ago, when it was the biggest city in the world. Three words for your pocket: the city's ruler was the *caliph* (KAY-liff), a kind of emperor, who kept a famous library called the House of Wisdom. The river through the city is the *Tigris* (TY-griss). And you'll hear the name *al-Khwarizmi* (al-KWAR-iz-mee) — a real mathematician whose book gave us the word "algebra." Ready? Here we go.

## The story

Salim's father held out the wrapped bundle with both hands, the way you hand someone an egg.

"One hundred sheets. The whitest paper this workshop has ever made, and Yusuf the bookseller is buying it for the caliph's own library. Carry it across the city, and let nothing happen to it."

"What could happen to it?"

"In Baghdad? Everything."

In those days, paper was nearly magic. Far to the west, people still wrote on stretched animal skin so costly that one book was worth as much as a house. But Baghdad had learned a secret from the roads to China: how to soak old linen rags into a soup of threads and press them into pale sheets that drank in ink and never let it go. Ink on skin could be scraped away by a cheat, Salim's father liked to say. Ink on paper was forever.

Salim walked carefully — past the date stalls, through streets that curved because the old heart of the city was a perfect circle with four great gates, out into the sprawl along the river Tigris where hundreds of thousands of people lived, more than anyone could count.

The trouble came at the bridge of boats. Halfway across the bobbing planks, a porter's donkey shied, knocked Salim sideways, and the bundle leapt from his arms. He caught it at the water's very edge — but the wrapper tore, and the wind stole the top sheet clean away over the river, and dropped the next one face-down in the mud.

Ninety-eight. He had been given a hundred, and he was bringing ninety-eight.

He walked into the booksellers' market like a boy going to a funeral — a hundred little shops of copyists and binders, his favorite place alive, and today he saw none of it.

Yusuf the bookseller unwrapped the bundle, looked at the muddy sheet, looked at Salim's face, and did not shout. "Tell me what happened. All of it, in order."

Salim told him. The donkey. The wind. The river.

"Hm." Yusuf turned to the scholar sitting cross-legged in the corner. "The palace ordered a hundred."

"Then we have a problem," said the scholar cheerfully, "and problems are my trade. I work in the caliph's library — where books come in Greek and Persian and from India, and we turn them into Arabic so everyone can read them. Muslims, Christians, Jews, all passing knowledge hand to hand like a bowl of dates. They even say one translator is paid the weight of his books in gold." He smiled. "I've never weighed them. But there's a man among us, al-Khwarizmi the mathematician, who taught me how problems are beaten. Not with worry — with steps. He wrote a whole book of it: how to split an inheritance fairly, how to balance what is owed against what is paid, step by step in plain words. He calls it *al-jabr* — restoring the balance. So. Step one: how many perfect sheets?"

They counted. Ninety-eight.

"Step two: what does the palace truly need this month?" Yusuf ran his finger down the ledger. "Ninety — for the astronomers. They're measuring the sky every night from the rooftops, writing down the stars. The caliph even sent men north to a flat plain to measure the size of the whole round Earth. Paced it, multiplied it. Step by step."

"Then ninety-eight is plenty," said the scholar, "and next month's batch fills the rest. Balance restored." He held up the mud-smeared sheet. "And this one I'll buy at half price — for my rough figuring, my mistakes, my almosts. Remember this, boy: every clean page of mathematics is built on a muddy page underneath."

Salim walked home light as the wind that had robbed him. That evening he climbed to the roof with his father and watched the city cool — swallows stitching the pink sky, the river turning to copper, and far across the water, small figures on a rooftop setting up brass instruments, waiting for the dark.

"They'll write tonight's stars on our paper," his father said.

"On ninety of our sheets," said Salim, who had learned to be exact.

The stars came out over Baghdad — the same stars that shone on China, where paper began. Somewhere below, a copyist finished a last line, and the paper drank the ink and kept it safe forever.

Salim went down to his mat and was asleep before the watchman passed the door — held all night, like a word in good ink, by the City of Peace.

## What's true in this story

- Salim, his father, Yusuf, and the scholar are invented — but papermakers, booksellers, and copyists like them were real, the booksellers' market really had a hundred or more shops, and bridges of boats really crossed the Tigris.
- Baghdad was founded in 762 CE as a perfectly round city with four gates, officially named the City of Peace. By the 800s it had grown far beyond its walls and was one of the largest cities in the world.
- Papermaking really arrived from China along the trade roads, and Baghdad had paper mills by the 790s. Clerks prized paper because ink soaked in and couldn't be scraped away and forged, unlike parchment.
- Caliph al-Ma'mun (ruled 813–833) kept the famous palace library tradition calls the House of Wisdom — historians think it was a library and circle of court scholars rather than a university — and the translation movement around it really drew Muslims, Christians, and Jews working side by side, turning Greek, Persian, and Indian works into Arabic.
- The tale that the translator Hunayn ibn Ishaq was paid the weight of his books in gold is a legend people told — so the scholar tells it that way.
- Al-Khwarizmi was real: his book on *al-jabr* (around 820 CE) gave us the word "algebra," and his name gave us "algorithm." He wrote his mathematics in words, step by step, for practical problems — the x's came centuries later.
- The caliph's astronomers really observed the sky nightly from Baghdad rooftops around 829–830, and his expedition to the plain of Sinjar really measured a degree of latitude to work out the size of the Earth — educated people in 830 knew perfectly well it was a sphere.
