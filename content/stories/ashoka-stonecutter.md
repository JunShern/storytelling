## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from India, more than two thousand two hundred years ago — a century before Julius Caesar. Most of India was ruled by one man: the emperor Ashoka (ah-SHOW-kah), whose armies had just won a terrible war against the coastal land of Kalinga (kah-LING-gah). Two more words: *dhamma* (DUM-mah) means right conduct — kindness, honesty, respect; and an *edict* (EE-dikt) is a ruler's official announcement. In our story, an emperor's words must travel without paper, printing, radio, or internet — carved into stone by hand. One stonecutter is about to get the strangest job of his life.

## The story

Deva had carved many things into rock: boundary lines, kings' boasts, the names of the dead. So when the emperor's officer unrolled the text he was to carve into the great rock above the road, Deva read it as a professional — and then he stopped, and read it again, and set down his chisel.

"This says the emperor is *sorry*."

"Keep your voice down," said the officer, though there was no one on the hill but goats.

"It says—" Deva read aloud, slowly, the way people read when they can hardly believe the words. "*When the Beloved of the Gods conquered Kalinga, one hundred and fifty thousand were carried away, one hundred thousand were slain... and the Beloved of the Gods felt remorse, for when an independent country is conquered, the slaughter and death of the people is extremely grievous to him.*" He looked up. "Emperors don't say this. Emperors say the gods smiled and the enemy wept. I've carved that one. Six times."

"This emperor," said the officer, "says this. On rocks and pillars from the mountains to the sea, in every language of every road. Where they speak Greek, in Greek. Where the scribes write the northwestern letters, in those. Here — in the people's own tongue, so that whoever passes can hear it."

"Hear it? Most folk can't read."

"That's why it's going beside the road, cutter, not in a palace. Travelers who read will read it aloud. That is the plan: every rock a voice."

Deva picked his chisel back up and turned it in his fingers. He was a Kalinga man. Everyone here was, or was married to one, or had buried one. The war was eight years past, and the land still had holes in it shaped like people.

"Why should I carve the apology of the man who did it?"

The officer was quiet for a moment, and when he spoke he sounded less like an officer.

"I asked something like that myself, once. Read the rest."

So Deva read the rest. The emperor's words went on: that henceforth he wished conquest only by dhamma — by right conduct, not by armies. That he had planted banyan trees along the roads for shade, and dug wells, and set up rest houses. That he had built healing houses with medicines — for people, and for animals too, which made Deva snort and then, reading it again, blink. That the royal kitchens, which once cooked hundreds of animals a day, now cooked almost none. That officers of dhamma now traveled the land with instructions to treat everyone gently. And this, which Deva read three times: *one should honor another man's religion, for by honoring it, one glorifies one's own.*

"He kept his army," Deva said at last, testing it.

"He did. He's an emperor, not a saint. He even warns the forest tribes he still has teeth." The officer shrugged. "I don't carry his heart in a box, cutter. I only know what I've seen: the wells are real. I've drunk from them. The shade trees are real; I've slept under them. The hospitals are real — my own mule was doctored at one, and my mother-in-law too, and both are thriving, worse luck. Words on a rock can lie. Wells can't."

Deva looked down the road, where it ran shimmering toward the sea — the road with its young banyans, planted at intervals, casting each its small circle of shade that would grow for a hundred years.

"My grandfather used to say," he said, "that a man is what he does twice. Anyone can be sorry once, loudly. But dig five hundred wells..."

He set the chisel against the stone.

"I'll carve it," he said. "Not for him. For whoever walks this road when he's dust and I'm dust. Let them stop in the shade and hear a strange thing: that the most powerful man in the world once looked at his greatest victory and called it his greatest sorrow. Maybe they'll be the kind of people who can hear that. Maybe hearing it will help them become so."

He began to cut. The letters marched out under his hands, round and patient, syllable by syllable — and among them, plain as a well by the road, that impossible sentence: *the Beloved of the Gods felt remorse.*

The chips of stone fell away like little birds, and the goats watched, and the sea wind came up the hill, and the words settled into the rock to wait — as it turned out — for the whole rest of history.

## What's true in this story

- Deva and the officer are invented, but Ashoka, the war, and the words are real. Around 261 BCE Ashoka conquered Kalinga (today's Odisha, on India's east coast); his own edict gives the terrible numbers — 100,000 killed, 150,000 deported — and then does what no other ancient ruler's inscription does: declares his remorse, in almost exactly the words Deva reads.
- The edicts are real and you can still visit them: messages carved on great rocks and on polished stone pillars across India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan — in local Prakrit dialects, in Greek and Aramaic near Kandahar — placed along roads and gathering places to be read aloud to those who couldn't read.
- The good works are all from the edicts: wells and banyan trees along the roads, rest houses, medical care established "for men and for animals," officers of dhamma, the royal kitchen's meat reduced to almost nothing, and the call to honor other people's religions.
- The story is honest about the limits: Ashoka kept his army and warned the forest tribes he could still use it, and historians debate how deep the transformation went. But he launched no more wars of conquest in thirty more years of rule.
- Ashoka calls himself "Beloved of the Gods" in the inscriptions; only one rock, found at Maski in 1915, adds his actual name — which is how scholars proved who the mysterious apologizing emperor was. The script itself had been forgotten for centuries until James Prinsep deciphered it in 1837.
- One of Ashoka's pillars was topped with four carved lions. Today that carving is the national emblem of India, and the wheel from its base is on India's flag — a Kalinga war's strange, long echo.
