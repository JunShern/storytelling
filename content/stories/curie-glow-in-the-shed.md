## Before we begin

Tonight's story happens in Paris, the capital of France, about a hundred and twenty-five years ago — when electric light was new and automobiles still startled the horses. Three names to meet: Marie Curie (mah-REE kew-REE), a scientist from Poland; her husband Pierre (pee-AIR); and their little daughter Irène (ee-REN), whose eyes we'll borrow. One word to learn: *radium* (RAY-dee-um) — a rare, mysterious substance the Curies discovered, hidden inside a heavy black rock called pitchblende. And one warning from the future: nobody yet knew radium could be dangerous. Keep that in your pocket. Now — to Paris.

## The story

Irène was five years old, and she had a list of questions.

"Grand-père," she asked her grandfather, who took care of her most days, "why does Maman come home smelling of smoke?"

"Because she stirs a great pot all day, like a soup witch," said Grand-père, who was a doctor and liked accurate answers dressed as jokes.

"What's in the pot?"

"Rock soup."

This was, as it turned out, exactly true.

One winter evening, Maman came home with rock-dust on her sleeves and a look on her face Irène had never seen before — tired underneath, but lit up on top, like a lamp behind a curtain.

"Would you like to see where I work, ma petite?" she asked. "Tonight, after dark?"

The place where Maman and Papa worked was not a palace of science. It was a wooden shed behind a school, with a glass roof that leaked when it rained and a stove that warmed nothing but itself. A visiting scientist had once looked at it and said it was a cross between a stable and a potato cellar. Maman told this story the way other people told jokes.

"Why do you work in a shed?" Irène asked, holding her mother's hand in the dark courtyard.

"Because no one would give us a better room," said Maman, simply. "And because the shed was free, and the question was important."

"What question?"

Maman crouched down to her height. "We found that a certain rock sends out rays — tiny, invisible arrows of energy — all by itself, forever, without fire, without sunlight, without anyone winding it up. Nobody can explain it. Inside that rock is something new, something no one has ever seen. I am cooking tonnes of rock to find one pinch of it."

"How big a pinch?"

"Smaller than a grain of rice. From a mountain of rock as heavy as four elephants." Maman smiled. "Papa says I am mad. Papa helps me anyway. That is what love is, by the way."

They had worked four years, she said. Maman stirred the boiling rock-broth with an iron rod nearly as tall as herself, batch after twenty-kilo batch, her arms aching, her notebooks filling with numbers. She had named the new thing already — *radium*, from the word for ray — but naming a thing is not the same as holding it, and scientists would not believe in it until she could put a speck of it on a scale.

"And now?" Irène asked.

"Now," said Maman, "we have our pinch." She pushed open the shed door. "Don't light the lamp."

The shed was full of fireflies.

That was Irène's first thought. All along the shelves and tables, little glass tubes and dishes were glowing in the dark — a pale, blue-green light, faint and steady, like moonlight that had been bottled and put away neatly, row after row.

"Oh," said Irène, which was the correct scientific response.

Papa's voice came from the dark, warm and pleased. "The glowing ones are our concentrations. The light never goes out. It needs no fuel. It simply — gives."

"Are they alive?"

"No, little one. But they are new. When your Maman was a girl in Poland, no book in the world had this in it. She had to find it first and write the book after." He lifted her up so she could see the brightest dish. "Your mother did this. Remember that when people tell you what girls cannot do."

They stood a long time in the dark, the three of them, watching the shelves shine. Maman said, very quietly, that the glowing tubes looked like faint fairy lights, and that on hard days she came in just to look at them.

Years later, the world would learn to be careful with radium — its invisible arrows could hurt as well as heal, and even Maman's notebooks would have to be kept in lead-lined boxes. But it would also learn to use those rays to see inside the body and to fight disease, and the little girl in the shed would grow up to be a scientist in her own right — and win the same great prize her mother won.

That night, though, she was just a sleepy five-year-old on her father's shoulder, going home through the gas-lit streets of Paris.

"Grand-père was right," she murmured. "Rock soup."

"The finest ever cooked," said Maman.

## What's true in this story

- Irène, Marie, Pierre, and Grand-père Eugène are all real. Marie Skłodowska Curie came from Warsaw, Poland, to study in Paris; Pierre's father, Dr. Eugène Curie, lived with the family and largely raised little Irène while her parents worked.
- The shed was real: a leaky wooden hangar with a glass roof behind the School of Physics and Chemistry on rue Lhomond. The visiting chemist Wilhelm Ostwald really described it as "a cross between a stable and a potato shed" — and could not believe great science was done there.
- The work was as heroic as described: from tonnes of pitchblende ore, processed roughly twenty kilograms at a time with Marie stirring a boiling mass with an iron rod, the Curies isolated about one tenth of a gram of radium chloride by 1902 — four years after they had announced radium must exist.
- The glow was real. Radium compounds shine faintly blue-green in the dark, and Marie wrote in her own memoir of coming to the shed at night to see their vials glowing like "faint, fairy lights."
- Nobody then understood the danger of radiation. Marie and Pierre worked unprotected; her notebooks are still radioactive today and are stored in lead-lined boxes. The story keeps this honest — the characters don't know what we know.
- In 1903 Marie and Pierre shared the Nobel Prize in Physics (with Henri Becquerel) — and it nearly went to the men only, until Pierre, tipped off by a sympathetic mathematician, insisted Marie's name be included. She was the first woman ever to win a Nobel Prize, and later won a second, in Chemistry.
- Irène really did grow up to be a scientist — and won her own Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1935, with her husband Frédéric Joliot, for discovering artificial radioactivity.
