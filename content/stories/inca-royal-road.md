## Before we begin

Tonight we run through the Andes (AN-deez) — the longest mountain range on Earth, down the western side of South America, in the country now called Peru. Five hundred years ago these mountains held the Inca (ING-kah) Empire, the largest in the Americas — an empire with no horses, no wheels, and no writing, that still sent messages faster than galloping Europe. How? The *chasqui* (CHAHS-kee): relay runners posted along the royal roads. Two more words: a *quipu* (KEE-poo) is a bundle of knotted cords that records numbers; a *pututu* (poo-TOO-too) is a conch-shell trumpet. Ready? Run.

## The story

The message left Cusco at dawn, and this story runs beside it.

The first runner was called Cusi, and he was fast the way rivers are fast — not hurrying, just unable to be slow. At the stone hut beside the road he waited on his toes, and when the runner from the city came pounding up the valley, Cusi heard him before he saw him: the moan of the pututu, the conch trumpet, blown on the run. That sound meant one thing. *Message coming. Start moving.*

Cusi began to run — slowly, along the road, so that the city runner drew level with him at a sprint and neither had to stop.

"For the north!" the runner gasped, matching stride. "Quipu and words both. The words: *The bridge at the great gorge is damaged. Send the bridge-masters. The north road must not close.* Say it back!"

"The bridge at the great gorge is damaged. Send the bridge-masters. The north road must not close!"

"Go!"

The quipu passed between them at a dead run — a fistful of knotted cords, its knots holding the counts: which bridge, how many workers, how urgent. The words rode in Cusi's mouth; the numbers rode in his hand. And the city runner dropped away behind, walking now, chest heaving, his part done.

Cusi ran. This was the Qhapaq Ñan, the royal road, and it did not believe in mountains. Stone steps up cliffs; causeways striding over marshes; and always ahead of him the road unrolling like a decision that had already been made. He ran his stretch — a few thousand paces, no more, for that is the secret; no man runs far, every man runs *fresh* — and as the next hut rose in sight, he filled his lungs and blew the pututu.

At the hut, a runner named Yauri was already moving, already matching him.

"The bridge at the great gorge is damaged! Send the bridge-masters! The north road must not close! Say it back!"

Yauri said it back, took the quipu in stride, and was gone up the pass like weather.

And so it went, hut by hut, all day and — this is the marvel — all night, torch-flames and starlight, the message never once stopping to rest, because no single runner ever grew tired. It slept in no bed. It went over passes where the air is thin as a whisper and runners chew coca leaves against the cold and the gasping. It crossed gorges on swaying bridges woven — woven! — from mountain grass, braided into cables thick as a man's leg, rebuilt fresh by the villages each year as their work-tax to the empire; for the Inca paid no coins in tribute, but labor, each village giving the empire so many days of hands.

Past the runners' huts stood the greater stations, the tambos, a day's walk apart, stocked with food and cloaks and firewood for official travelers. Past the tambos, terraced fields stepped up the mountainsides like green staircases. The road saw everything and the message saw nothing. It was two days old now, and it had gone farther than a horseman in Europe could dream, and it said only, urgently, over and over, in relays of borrowed breath: *the bridge — the bridge-masters — the road must not close.*

On the third morning, in a northern town, the last runner sprinted into the governor's courtyard, and said the words in a voice not his own — the voice, really, of the messenger in Cusco days ago and mountains away — and put the quipu into the hands of the quipu-keeper, who read the knots with his fingers like a man playing music backward.

"Bridge-masters!" the governor called, before the runner had even caught his breath. "Gather your ropes. The great gorge. *Run.*"

They say — the old chroniclers who wrote it down afterward — that the relay could carry a message a hundred and fifty miles in a day; that news could run the length of the empire, Cusco to Quito, in about a week; that the emperor in his mountain capital could dine on fish that had left the ocean, in a runner's pack, only two days before. They say a chasqui's sons trained by racing their fathers uphill. What is certain is this: with no horse, no wheel, and no written word, the Incas moved news across one of the fiercest landscapes on Earth faster than anyone else in their world — with legs, lungs, knots, and trust.

Trust most of all. That is the part to sleep on. Cusi never learned what became of the bridge — he had run his stretch and gone home to supper. No runner ever saw the whole journey. Each one simply believed that the next would be standing at his hut, ready, listening for the conch; and each one was; and so a whisper crossed an empire.

Away south, the bridge-masters reached the gorge, and the grass cables creaked, and the work began. The road did not close.

The road, in fact, is still there. Walkers find its stones under the grass of five countries. And in one valley in Peru, to this day, the villages still gather every year to weave a bridge of grass across the gorge, each family bringing its ropes — because their great-grandmothers did, and because some things should not be allowed to stop.

## What's true in this story

- Cusi, Yauri, and this particular message are invented; everything they do is real. The chasqui relay system carried messages along the Inca royal roads by short sprints between roadside huts, with the incoming runner sounding a pututu (conch trumpet) so the next runner could start moving and take the handoff at speed, repeating the message aloud.
- The Qhapaq Ñan ("royal road") was a genuine wonder: a network of roughly 30,000–40,000 kilometers of engineered roads through the Andes and along the coast, with stone stairways, causeways, and suspension bridges. It's now a UNESCO World Heritage Site spanning six countries.
- The speeds come from Spanish chroniclers who saw the system working: on the order of 150 miles (240 km) a day, Cusco to Quito in about a week, and fresh coastal fish carried to the emperor in Cusco in around two days — the story hedges these with "they say," as historians do.
- The Incas really had no writing, no horses, and no wheeled vehicles. Messages traveled as memorized words plus a quipu — knotted cords encoding numbers and records, read by trained keepers. Coca-leaf chewing at altitude and labor-tax (mit'a) instead of coin tribute are both accurate.
- The grass suspension bridges are real: cables braided from twisted mountain grass, rebuilt annually by local communities as part of their labor duty. One — the Q'eswachaka bridge in Peru — is still rewoven by hand every year by the surrounding villages, five centuries on.
- Tambos — supply stations spaced roughly a day's walk apart, stocked for official travelers — lined the roads exactly as described. Ordinary people, incidentally, couldn't use the royal roads freely; they were imperial infrastructure, which is why our message travels so unimpeded.
