## Before we begin

Tonight we sail from ancient Egypt — nearly three and a half thousand years ago, a thousand years before ancient Rome. Egypt's ruler is Hatshepsut (hat-SHEP-soot), a woman who rules as pharaoh — as king — one of the very few ever to do so. She is sending five ships to a far southern land called Punt (rhymes with "shunt") to bring back *myrrh* (MUR) — a precious perfume that comes from the sap of a desert tree, burned in temples like incense. Our storyteller keeps the ship's log — the captain's daily record. The fleet commander is Nehsi (NEH-see). Anchors up.

## The story

**Season of the Harvest, day 4.** I am scribe of the ship *Star of the Two Lands*, and this is our log. Today the impossible order arrived, sealed with the seal of Pharaoh herself. We are to sail to Punt — Punt! — the land at the far end of the sea, which trades its treasures up the coast hand to hand, but which no Egyptian fleet has visited in living memory. Grandmothers tell of it like a fairy tale. Pharaoh Hatshepsut says her god has commanded her to do a thing no ruler has done: not merely to buy Punt's myrrh, but to bring back the *living trees*, roots and all, and plant a garden of them before the temple. The sailors laughed, then read the order again, and stopped laughing.

**Day 11.** Here is the maddest part, which I record so my grandchildren will believe it. Our ships were built on the Nile — but the Nile does not reach the eastern sea. So we have taken the ships *apart*. Five ships, every plank numbered, loaded onto donkeys — hundreds of donkeys — and now we walk the desert road east, carrying our fleet in pieces. Eight days of sand, each man with his water ration, the planks creaking on the pack-saddles like a forest complaining. The commander Nehsi rides at the front, calm as a statue. When I asked him if it could be done, he said: "It has been done before, scribe. The sea road is old. We are only waking it up."

**Day 23.** At the shore camp we put our ships back together like great wooden puzzles, sewing and pegging, tarring the seams. Today the *Star* floated whole again, thirty rowers to a side, her one great sail spread. The men cheered her like a returning mother. South now, with the wind behind us.

**Day 41.** Sailing south along a coast of red mountains. The sea here swarms with fish of shapes I have no words for, so I have drawn them in the margin instead — spotted ones, flat round ones, one like a spear with wings. The artists aboard draw everything. Pharaoh commanded: record all of it, that it may be carved in stone at home.

**Day 52. PUNT.** We have arrived, and I must write carefully, for no one will believe any of this. The people of Punt live in round houses set on stilts, with little ladders to climb up, among myrrh trees and ebony and gold. Their chief came to meet us with his wife, the lady Ati, and — wonder — they did not fight us, nor we them. Nehsi spread out our trade goods on the beach, and the bargaining began like a festival. They have never seen the like of our goods; we have never seen the like of theirs. No coins pass — Egypt has no coins, nor do they; we trade thing for thing, and both sides go away certain they have done better than the other, which is the sign of a fair bargain.

**Day 60.** The trees. Thirty-one myrrh trees, dug with their roots wrapped in great balls of their own earth, each set in a basket that four men — or two very insulted men — can carry on poles. We water them like royal children. The captain has threatened terrible things to anyone who lets a goat near them. Also aboard: myrrh resin in heaps, gold, ebony, ivory, and baboons, who have opinions about everything and have already stolen my second-best pen.

**Day 94.** Home waters. The trees live. The baboons thrive, worse luck.

**Final entry.** Today I saw a thing I will tell until I die. Up the great river to Thebes came our fleet, and the whole city stood on the banks to see the trees of Punt sail by. Pharaoh Hatshepsut herself received the count of treasures, and the myrrh trees went into the earth before her great terraced temple — a row of green ambassadors from the end of the world, growing in Egypt.

And Pharaoh commanded the whole voyage carved on her temple walls: the ships, the stilt houses, the lady Ati, the fish I drew — everything. "Let it be seen," she said, "for as long as the stones stand."

Scribe's note, added at the bottom, small: *They will say a woman cannot rule as king. The trees say otherwise. Sleep well, ships. The sea road is awake again.*

## What's true in this story

- The scribe is invented, but the voyage is real: around Year 9 of Pharaoh Hatshepsut's reign (roughly 1470 BCE), she sent a five-ship expedition to Punt under a commander named Nehsi. We know it because she did exactly what the story says — had the whole journey carved on the walls of her temple at Deir el-Bahri, where you can still see it today.
- Hatshepsut really was a woman who ruled as pharaoh — one of very few in three thousand years of Egyptian history — and the Punt voyage was among her proudest achievements.
- The portage is real: Egyptian seagoing ships were built on the Nile, disassembled, carried about 150 kilometers across the desert to the Red Sea, and reassembled. Archaeologists found the harbor camp at Mersa Gawasis — complete with stored ship timbers and coils of rope. The donkeys are historical; camels weren't yet used there.
- The carvings really show Punt's stilt houses with ladders, the chief's wife Queen Ati, heaps of myrrh, ebony, gold, ivory, baboons — and Red Sea fish drawn so accurately that modern scientists can identify the species.
- The thirty-one living myrrh trees, roots balled in baskets, are shown on the temple wall — history's first recorded plant-collecting expedition. Trade was barter; neither Egypt nor Punt used coins.
- Where exactly Punt lay was debated for centuries. In 2020, scientists studied a mummified baboon brought back from Punt and found it had grown up in the region of modern Eritrea/Ethiopia/Somalia — the Horn of Africa, just as the story assumes.
- Hatshepsut herself stayed home — pharaohs sent expeditions rather than sailing along — and hers wasn't the first Egyptian voyage to Punt, only the best documented. The "sea road" really was old, and she really did wake it up.
