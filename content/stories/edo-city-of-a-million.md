## Before we begin

Tonight we're going to Japan, three hundred years ago, to a city called Edo (EH-doh) — the city that is now Tokyo. Around 1700 it held about a million people, which may have made it the biggest city on Earth. Two words before we start: an *apprentice* is a child learning a trade by living and working in a shop, and *soba* (SOH-bah) are buckwheat noodles, slurped loudly — which is the polite way. And one fact the whole story turns on: Edo was built almost entirely of wood and paper, so the thing its people feared most, and were best at fighting, was fire. Listen for the bell.

## The story

The fire bell woke Kichi out of a dream about noodles.

*Kan! Kan! Kan!* — fast and hard from the watchtower, and the head clerk was standing over him with a lantern, already dressed.

"Up, boy. Fire in the ward north of the bridge. Wind's from the north."

Kichi was ten, the shop's newest apprentice, and he knew what wind from the north meant. The fire would walk south. Toward the bridge. Toward the shop.

"Is it far?" he asked, scrambling into his jacket.

"Far is nothing," said the clerk, "in a city made of wood. Ledgers first! The books, then the best cloth. The building can burn — the *accounts* must not."

"The building can *burn*?"

"Buildings grow back. This city has burned and grown back more times than you have teeth. Why do you think we joke that fires are the flowers of Edo? A terrible joke — we laugh so we don't cry. My grandmother lived through the great fire: half the city gone, the castle's tall tower gone, never rebuilt. Now *move*."

Outside, the street was a river of people and handcarts, moving fast but hardly anyone screaming. Neighbors counted each other's children; an old woman directed traffic with a broom. Half a million commoners lived packed in these low wards, and it was as if the whole crowded city had rehearsed.

Then came the firemen — one of the great lords' brigades, running under their banner, and the crowd squeezed aside. Kichi watched them swarm onto the roofs of houses that weren't burning, and begin tearing them down.

"Why those houses?" he shouted. "They're not on fire!"

"Watch," said the clerk, pushing the cart. "There's no water in the world enough for a fire like that. So we don't fight it with water — we take away its food. The fire arrives at its own feast and finds the table cleared. Those people's houses will be rebuilt within the month, by all of us. Grass burns, boy, and grass grows back. Edo is grass."

They stood the night in the safe-ground by the river, and the clerk — who seemed to know Kichi needed talking to as much as a blanket — talked. About the water that came to the city's wells from hills far away, through a channel dug by two clever brothers, then under the streets in pipes of hollowed wood. About how nothing in Edo was wasted: old paper mashed into new paper, candle stubs melted into candles, ash sold to the dyers, old clothes resold — even what a million people left in the privies each morning *sold*, for good money, to farmers who carted it out to feed the vegetable fields.

"A city that wastes nothing can grow forever. A million people — maybe the most in the world. Half of them samurai up on the hills, who haven't fought a war in nearly a hundred years and file papers all day, poor souls, while we merchants quietly run the place." He glanced down. "Don't repeat that."

Toward dawn the wind swung east, and the word came down the street like water down a dry ditch: *the gap held.* The fire had starved exactly where the firemen had cleared, three wards short of the bridge.

The shop stood untouched. And at midday the clerk put four small coins in Kichi's hand and walked him to the soba stall, where they slurped their noodles standing up, as loudly as possible, while the street sang its ordinary songs around them — the tofu-seller's tune, the clam-seller's, the goldfish man's, every trade with its own little melody, so that a blind man could shop by ear in Edo, and often did.

"You did well, small one," the clerk said. "Ledgers first. You'll make a merchant yet."

That night, scrubbed pink from the bathhouse, Kichi lay on his futon and listened: the last wooden sandals clacking over the bridge, a dog barking — safely, for by the shogun's own law nobody in Edo might harm even a stray dog, which the dogs all seemed to know — and to the north, faint and steady, saws and hammers, where a burned ward was already growing back.

The watchman came down the alley, knocking his wooden clappers. *Tok, tok.* All is well. Fires sleeping. City sleeping.

Grass grows back, thought Kichi, nearly asleep. A million blades of it, breathing together in the dark.

*Tok, tok.*

He slept.

## What's true in this story

- Kichi and the clerk are invented, but boys really did enter merchant shops as live-in apprentices around age ten, and saving the ledgers first in a fire was real merchant practice.
- Around 1700 Edo really held roughly a million people — possibly the most on Earth — about half samurai households, drawn by the law requiring every lord to live in Edo in alternate years, after nearly a century of peace.
- Fires really were called "the flowers of Edo," and the Great Meireki Fire of 1657 destroyed most of the city and the castle keep, which was never rebuilt. Around 1700, firefighting was done mainly by the lords' brigades, who fought fire chiefly by tearing down buildings in its path — starving the flames, not dousing them.
- The water system is real: the Tamagawa aqueduct, dug in the 1650s by the Tamagawa brothers, ran over forty kilometers and fed neighborhood wells through underground wooden pipes.
- Edo's recycling is real and astonishing — paper, wax, ash, and old clothes all bought and resold, and farmers genuinely paid for night soil to fertilize the fields — one reason Edo was far cleaner than the European capitals of its day.
- Street vendors really sold everything with their own cries and songs; soba really cost about sixteen mon a bowl (sushi as we know it came a century later); and the shogun Tsunayoshi's "Laws of Compassion" really did protect stray dogs — Edoites found the law as funny, and as inconvenient, as Kichi's dog seems to.
