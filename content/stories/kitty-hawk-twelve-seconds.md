## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from the United States — from Kitty Hawk, a windy strip of sand on the coast of North Carolina — in December 1903, about a hundred and twenty years ago. Two brothers from Ohio, Wilbur and Orville Wright (RITE), bicycle makers by trade, have spent four years trying to build a flying machine. Our storyteller is John Daniels (DAN-yuls), a real lifesaver from the local rescue station — a man who, on the morning of our story, had never seen an airplane *or* a camera. By nightfall he'd be famous for both. Listen — he's telling it his way.

## The story

Folks are always asking me about that morning, so I'll tell it the way I'd swear to it, because I did swear to it, more than once.

It was the seventeenth of December, 1903, and it was *cold* — the puddles frozen, and the wind coming over the sand at better than twenty miles an hour. Now, we boys at the lifesaving station knew the Wright brothers. Two quiet fellows from Ohio in neckties and starched collars, out on the dunes at all hours, flying their big white gliders like kites. Some folks reckoned they were fools. We reckoned they were the workingest men we ever saw — when they wanted something done, they did it, and if it broke, they mended it and tried again.

That morning they hung a signal flag on their shed, which meant: *come and help, and come and watch.* Five of us went — four men and a boy named Johnny Moore. Five witnesses, that's all, for the whole of it.

The machine sat on its little wooden rail on the flat sand. It didn't look like much and it looked like everything: two great cloth wings, a motor they'd built themselves, two propellers turning behind. The brothers had flipped a coin days before for first turn — Wilbur won, tried on the fourteenth, and came down hard in three seconds. So now it was Orville's go.

Before he climbed on, Orville set up a box on three wooden legs, pointed it at the end of the rail, and looked at me.

"Mr. Daniels," he says, "when the machine reaches that point, squeeze this bulb."

"What is it?"

"A camera. It makes a picture."

Well, I had never seen a camera in my life. I'd never seen a *picture get made*. And he's handing me a little rubber bulb on a hose like it was nothing, and climbing onto the bottom wing, and lying down flat across it with his hips in a cradle.

The two of them stood together a minute, and shook hands — and us boys couldn't help noticing they held on a little long, like two fellows who weren't sure they'd meet again.

Then the motor set to popping and the propellers spun and the whole contraption shivered like a wet dog, and at half past ten it started down the rail with Wilbur running alongside holding the wing steady, and I stood there gripping that bulb with my heart going like the motor, and right at the end of the rail —

it *lifted.*

Wheels — no, it had no wheels — skids, cloth, motor, man and all, it rose up off this earth into the wind, and I squeezed the bulb. Didn't aim. Didn't think. Just squeezed, the way he told me.

It flew rough, like a colt let out of the barn — up a little, down a little, bucking the gusts — and came to rest on the sand about a hundred and twenty feet out. Twelve seconds, start to finish. I know it doesn't sound like much. Fifty-seven ways I've heard folk make it small: shorter than this, slower than that. But I'll tell you what it was. It was the first time in the history of the world a machine carrying a man raised itself by its own power into the air and flew. Twelve seconds that had never happened before, not once, since the earth was made.

We didn't holler much. Mostly we stood there. Then we ran to carry her back, and they flew her three times more, turn and turn about, and the last go Wilbur kept her up fifty-nine seconds and made it over eight hundred feet.

And then, while we stood talking, a gust got under the machine and flipped it, and — I may as well own it — it took me with it. I grabbed hold to weigh her down and went over inside her, tangled up in wires and cloth and motor, rolling over and over. Tore the machine to pieces; bruised me all colors. The boys laughed that I'd just survived the world's first airplane wreck. I've called myself the proudest man about it ever since.

Here's the part I love best. That evening they walked to town and telegraphed their father: four flights, longest fifty-nine seconds, home for Christmas. And the newspapers mostly didn't print it — one editor said if it had been fifty-seven *minutes*, then it might've been worth a line. Nobody believed it, you see. Except six men on a cold beach who saw it.

And the picture? Some weeks on, they got that glass plate home to Ohio and developed it — and there it was. Sharp as morning. The machine in the air, Wilbur mid-stride beside it, the whole first second of the age of flight. First photograph I ever took. They tell me it might be the most famous photograph in the world.

Wasn't luck, though, what them boys did. It was hard work and horse sense. They believed, and they worked, and the world changed on a Thursday.

Now go to sleep. And if you dream of flying — well, somebody had to be first.

## What's true in this story

- John T. Daniels was real — a crewman at the Kill Devil Hills lifesaving station — and everything here follows his sworn and spoken accounts. He really had never seen a camera before Orville positioned it, handed him the bulb, and told him when to squeeze.
- The first flight happened on 17 December 1903: freezing weather, wind over twenty miles an hour, Orville aboard at 10:35 a.m., about 120 feet in 12 seconds. There were exactly five witnesses, summoned by a signal flag — Daniels, Willie Dough, Adam Etheridge, W.C. Brinkley, and young Johnny Moore.
- Wilbur had won the coin toss for the first attempt on 14 December and stalled after about three seconds — which is why 17 December was Orville's turn. The brothers took turns: four flights that day, the last one Wilbur's, 852 feet in 59 seconds.
- The gust that wrecked the Flyer afterward really did carry Daniels head over heels inside the machine — he called himself the first man ever to survive an airplane wreck, and "the proudest man" about it. The Flyer never flew again.
- The telegram home really was garbled — it printed "57" seconds instead of 59 and misspelled Orville's name — and the press mostly ignored or disbelieved the news at first.
- The photograph is real and genuinely famous: Daniels's single squeeze of the bulb, developed weeks later in Dayton, captured the exact moment of liftoff. It was the first photograph he ever took.
- His closing judgment is close to his own recorded words from 1927: it wasn't luck — "it was hard work and common sense." And one strange, true footnote: John Daniels died in 1948, one day after Orville Wright.
