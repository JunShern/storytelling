## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from South Africa, from just thirty years ago — recent enough that millions who lived it can tell it themselves. For most of a century, South Africa was ruled under a system called apartheid (uh-PART-hate), which means "apartness": Black South Africans — most of the country — could not choose where to live, and had never once been allowed to vote for their nation's government. One name you may already know: Nelson Mandela (man-DEL-ah), who spent twenty-seven years in prison for fighting apartheid, and walked out, unbroken, to help end it peacefully. Today is April 27th, 1994 — voting day.

## The story

Gogo Nomvula — *gogo* is what everyone calls a grandmother — woke before the roosters, which she considered only fair, since she had been waiting seventy-six years and the roosters had waited for nothing.

"You don't have to be first, Gogo," her granddaughter Zanele said, wrapping her in a blanket against the autumn dark. "The voting goes all day. There are special hours for the elderly."

"Special hours." Gogo snorted, pinning her church hat. "Child, I was born in this country. I have carried a passbook like a criminal in my own streets. I have been told which door, which bench, which beach, which job, which *everything* — my whole life, told. Today nobody tells me. Today I stand in the line I choose. Fetch my good shoes."

The line, when they reached the school, was already astonishing. It ran from the gate down the hill, around the field, out of sight — and it was still filling, from every direction, as the sun came up: farm workers and nurses, old men in their army-straight rows, mothers with babies tied on their backs, white neighbors and Black neighbors standing in one queue, which Gogo looked at for a long moment without any comment except a slow nod. From the news helicopters, they said later, the lines across the country looked like rivers — dark, patient rivers, winding through fields and townships, millions of people long. Some people would wait four hours, some eight, some all day and into the next. Almost no one left.

"They say on the radio," said the man ahead of them, tilting his little transistor, "that Mandela is voting this morning. Near Durban — Ohlange school, at Inanda."

"Why there?" asked Zanele. "Why not Johannesburg?"

The man smiled and turned the radio up. Because, the announcer was explaining, at that school was the grave of John Dube — the very first president, eighty years ago, of Mandela's movement: a teacher who had begun the long walk when the law still pretended it would listen. Before voting, the radio said, Mandela had stood at the old man's grave and spoken to him quietly. Witnesses heard him say: *Mr. President, I have come to report to you that South Africa is now free.*

The line was silent for a moment, the way church is silent.

"Twenty-seven years in prison," said the man with the radio, to no one in particular. "And he comes out and shakes hands with the men who kept him there. And now he queues to vote like the rest of us."

"He does not queue," said Gogo, with authority. "He is seventy-five. There are special hours for the elderly." And the whole line around them laughed, and someone began, softly, to sing.

That was the day, people said afterward — you sang, or you were quiet, and both felt like the same thing. There was no fighting anywhere, though everyone had feared it; the country had bled badly on the road to this day, and bombs had gone off that very week, set by men who wanted the old world back. But the lines only grew longer and calmer, as if the whole nation had privately agreed: *not today. Today we are busy.*

It took five hours to reach the school hall. Inside, an official — a young woman whose hands shook a little with the size of her job — checked Gogo's hand under a lamp for the invisible ink that marked those who had voted, found none, and marked her.

"You know how, Gogo?" she whispered.

"I have practiced," said Gogo, "since before your mother was born."

The ballot was a long paper strip, and it was beautiful — every party listed with its name, its symbol, and a photograph of its leader, so that a person who had never been allowed to learn reading could still choose freely. No one may see your choice: that too, the young woman explained, was the law. Gogo went behind the cardboard screen, alone — alone with the paper, the little pencil, and seventy-six years — and made her X, and folded the ballot the way one folds a christening gown.

Into the box it went, with a sound like a heartbeat. *Thup.*

"Now," said Gogo Nomvula, straightening her hat, "I am a citizen. Take my photograph by the door, child. Your grandchildren will want proof I existed."

Far to the east, at Ohlange, Mandela had folded his own ballot — the first vote of his whole life, too; he was as new to this as Gogo — and dropped it in the box with a smile the cameras would carry around the world, and said that South Africa was beginning a new era: from pessimism and division, he said, toward hope, reconciliation, and the building of one nation.

The counting took days — nearly twenty million ballots, from those endless patient rivers of people. Mandela's party won by a landslide, and on the tenth of May he stood at the government buildings in Pretoria — where his vote had once been a crime — and was sworn in as President of South Africa, with the whole world watching and old enemies seated side by side.

Gogo Nomvula watched the inauguration on television with her good shoes on, "out of respect," and fell asleep in her chair before the speeches ended, which was also, everyone agreed, exactly right.

Every April 27th since, South Africa keeps a holiday. It is called Freedom Day. It is the anniversary of a queue.

Some lines are worth every hour of the wait. Sleep now — and may all your longest lines end at an open door.

## What's true in this story

- Gogo Nomvula and Zanele are invented; the day is real. On 26–29 April 1994, South Africa held its first democratic election — the first in which Black South Africans, the great majority of the country, could vote for their national government at all. Nearly twenty million ballots were cast, and the queues really did stretch for hours, sometimes kilometers, photographed from the air like winding rivers. Election days were remarkably peaceful, though right-wing bombs had killed people that very week.
- Mandela's morning happened as the radio describes: he voted — for the first time in his life, at age 75 — at Ohlange High School in Inanda, near Durban, chosen because its founder, John Dube, first president of the ANC, is buried there. At the graveside he reported, in substance: "Mr. President, I have come to report to you that South Africa is now free." (Accounts vary slightly in wording, so the story says "witnesses heard.")
- His words after voting are from his official statement that day, closely paraphrased: the country was moving from "an era of pessimism, division... turmoil and conflict" to "a new era of hope, reconciliation and nation building."
- The voting details are accurate: ballots listed each party with symbol and leader's photograph so non-readers could choose; hands were checked under ultraviolet light for invisible ink; and the elderly and disabled had a special early voting day (Gogo declines it on principle, as many did).
- Mandela really spent 27 years in prison (about 18 on Robben Island) and was released on 11 February 1990; the four years between release and election were dangerous and bloody — the story doesn't pretend otherwise. The ANC won 62.65 percent; Mandela was inaugurated on 10 May 1994 at the Union Buildings in Pretoria, with former president F.W. de Klerk as one of his deputies.
- April 27 is now a national holiday: Freedom Day — the anniversary, truly, of a queue.
