## Before we begin

Tonight's story happens in the middle of the Pacific — the widest ocean on Earth — about a thousand years ago. The Polynesians were the greatest ocean explorers the world had yet seen, settling islands scattered across a triangle of sea bigger than all of Europe. Two words for the voyage: the *canoe* here is no rowboat — it means a great double ship, two hulls joined by a deck, big enough for whole families; and the *navigator* is the one who finds the way, with no compass, no map, and no clock, because none existed there. Our girl is Hina (HEE-nah). The tide is right — let's go.

## The story

On the nineteenth day, the drinking water went to half-rations, and Hina heard her mother and the navigator talking low by the steering paddle.

"The children are asking how long," her mother said.

"Tell them: the land is close," said the navigator — Hina's great-uncle, an old man with eyes like a bird's.

"Is that true?"

A pause, with just the rush of two hulls through the swells and the creak of the coconut-fiber lashings that held their whole world together — for the great canoe had not one nail in her, only planks sewn tight with cord and ten thousand knots.

"It is almost true. In two days I will know."

They had sailed from home with everything a new world would need: bundled shoots of taro and banana and breadfruit wrapped in damp moss against the salt, chickens, a pig, a dog with opinions about the pig — and children. They were sailing east, into the rising wind, toward islands the old songs said were there, where no person had ever stood.

In the morning Hina planted herself in front of her great-uncle. "Teach me what you'll know in two days. If I'm going to be worried, I want to be worried about the right things."

He laughed until he had to sit down. "Good. Fear that asks questions is called sense. Now — what do I carry that finds land, and where do I keep it?"

Everyone knew that one. "In your head."

"In my head. The whole rim of the sea, divided into houses, and I know which star climbs from each house, all night, all year — a hundred stars and more. When one rises too high to trust, I take the next from the same house. That compass cannot be dropped overboard." He tapped his temple. "And when clouds cover every star, I feel the swells — great waves rolling for days from far winds, each train keeping its direction like a promise. I hold their angle against the hulls and steer blind, and I am not lost."

That night he made her lie flat on the deck, palms down. "Stop listening with your ears. Listen with your back." And after a long time she felt it — under the choppy fuss of the little waves, a slow deep lift-and-fall, patient as a grandmother's breathing.

"The old swell from the home wind," he said. "You see? The ocean is never confused. Only sailors are."

But the next day the wind faltered, the sun stood on them like a hot stone, and the water sloshed low in the gourds. Hina asked, very quietly: "What if the songs are wrong? What if there are no islands?"

He didn't scold her, which frightened her more than scolding.

"Then we turn and run home on the wind at our backs. That is why we sail *into* the wind, child — out is hard so that home is easy. We are never lost. But before we ever turn, we listen. Because an island is bigger than itself."

"Bigger than itself?"

"An island sends out messengers. Birds — the noddies and white terns sleep on land and fish at sea; mark them at dusk, for they fly home, and home is where we're going. Clouds — one that stands still while its brothers drift has an island under it, and over a green lagoon its belly glows green. Driftwood. A change in the swell. Read all the messengers together, and an island reaches out to meet you across thirty miles of water."

That evening a squall found them in the dark — a kind one, fat with rain, and the crew whooped and spread the sails and mats to catch it, the water drumming into the gourds until they hung heavy and sweet again, while Hina stood with her face up and her mouth open, drinking the sky.

And at dawn on the twenty-first day — exactly when he had promised to know — the old man touched her shoulder and pointed. Far ahead, small as a knuckle, a cloud was standing still. Under it, as the light grew, two white terns beat steadily out from the horizon, straight over the canoe, as if to say: *this way, neighbors. You're expected.*

The crew didn't cheer, which surprised Hina most of all. Her mother wept quietly; her great-uncle closed his bird-bright eyes for one long breath. Then came the work: a reef to read, a pass to find, a new world to carry ashore, seedling by seedling, chicken by outraged chicken.

That night they slept on land no person had ever slept on. Hina lay by the fire, listening to the surf — the sound the island had been making, all alone, for a million years, waiting for someone to hear it.

"Great-uncle. The next island. Will you teach me the stars for it?"

"Beginning tomorrow," he said.

And the ocean — the great road, the never-confused — breathed in and out on the reef, all night, like a grandmother watching children sleep.

## What's true in this story

- Hina and her family are invented, but voyages exactly like this happened: around 1,000–800 years ago, Polynesian voyagers deliberately explored and settled the last uninhabited islands on Earth — Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and finally Aotearoa (New Zealand) — a triangle of ocean bigger than all of Europe.
- The double-hulled voyaging canoes are real: built without metal, lashed with braided coconut-fiber cord, sailing at four to six knots, with whole families and "canoe plants" — taro, breadfruit, banana wrapped against the spray — plus chickens, pigs, and dogs.
- Navigation truly used no instruments. The "star compass" is a memorized map of where a hundred or more stars rise and set; steering in cloud by the feel of ocean swells through the hulls is a genuine technique still practiced today.
- The island "messengers" are real: land-nesting birds that fly home at dusk, clouds that stand over islands, lagoon-green light on a cloud's belly — together they make an island findable from tens of miles off. Crews really caught squall rain in sails and mats.
- Exploring into the wind so the way home would be easy is exactly how scholars believe it was done; the old idea that Polynesians drifted about by accident has been thoroughly disproved. Most researchers now think they even reached South America and returned — the sweet potato grew across Polynesia centuries before Europeans arrived, and DNA studies support the contact.
- We know the old methods work because they live again: in 1976 the canoe Hōkūle'a sailed Hawai'i to Tahiti — 2,500 miles — navigated without instruments by Mau Piailug of Satawal, Micronesia, who taught a new generation. Hōkūle'a has since sailed around the world the same way.
