## Before we begin

Tonight's story sails the Indian Ocean six hundred years ago, from Africa to China. China's Ming (MING) emperor has sent out the greatest fleet the world has ever seen — hundreds of ships, tens of thousands of sailors — commanded by the admiral Zheng He (jung HUH). These are not warships hunting conquest; they carry silk and porcelain, and collect wonders. Two more words: *Malindi* (mah-LIN-dee) is a trading city on the coast of Kenya, in East Africa; and a *qilin* (chee-LIN) is a gentle magical creature of Chinese legend — a kind of unicorn — said to appear only when a land is wisely ruled. Now, to the harbor.

## The story

Jomo's giraffe was named Twiga, and Twiga had just been given away to the most powerful man on Earth.

"They can't," Jomo said, for the tenth time, on the beach at Malindi. "She won't eat for strangers."

"Which is why you're going with her," said his uncle, and pointed at the sea.

The horizon had grown ships. All week they had been arriving — not ships such as the coast knew, the swift dhows and traders, but floating towns, junks with sails like red mountain ranges, so many and so vast that fishermen simply turned around and went home to fetch their families to look. The treasure fleet of the Ming had come across the whole ocean, and its boats now rowed ashore carrying silk the color of sunsets and porcelain so fine you could see tea-shadow through it. In exchange, the king of Malindi was sending ambassadors — and a gift to stagger an emperor.

Twiga, chewing, eighteen feet tall, entirely unimpressed.

Getting a giraffe onto a ship is a story in itself, involving a padded stall built to order on the deck, a ramp, forty men with ropes they were afraid to pull, and finally Jomo simply walking up the ramp ahead of her with a branch of sweet acacia, as he had led her since she was a wobbling calf. She followed him the way she always did — like a duchess doing a favor.

The voyage was weeks upon weeks. Jomo, who had never left Malindi, now lived inside a wooden city. The great ship had crews beyond counting, and everything had its office: cooks, carpenters, sailmakers, interpreters who between them spoke the languages of every shore, and doctors — the fleet carried whole colleges of doctors. Navigators steered by a fish-shaped needle floating in a bowl of water, which always pointed south, and by burning incense sticks to measure the hours, and at night by the stars, holding carved boards at arm's length against the horizon to read how far north they'd come.

An old interpreter named Ma took to visiting Twiga's stall at dusk, practicing his Swahili on Jomo and correcting his chopsticks.

"You should know what she is sailing into," Ma said one evening. "The court has decided your Twiga is a qilin."

"A what?"

"A creature from the oldest legends. Gentle. Eats no flesh, harms no living grass. It appears — so it is said — only when Heaven is pleased, when the land is ruled with virtue." Ma's mouth twitched. "The emperor has been told his virtue has summoned it from the ends of the earth."

Jomo looked at Twiga, who was trying to eat the rigging.

"She's not a unicorn. She's a giraffe. We have herds of them."

"I know that. You know that. I suspect, between us, the emperor knows it too." Ma lowered his voice, comfortable as a man settling into a favorite story. "Understand: our emperor took his throne in a war, from his own nephew. Some whisper that Heaven frowns on him. So when a beast out of legend walks off a ship in front of ten thousand people — that is worth more than a hundred proclamations. Wonders are politics, boy. Eat your rice."

Jomo worried the whole way north. At the docks the crowds were beyond anything — and Twiga, who hated noise, leaned down and took the acacia calmly from his hand, and the crowds sighed like a tide. In the capital they walked her, Jomo at her halter in his best coat, through gates within gates, into a courtyard of such silence and splendor that even Twiga stood still.

The Emperor of the Ming looked at the qilin for a long time.

Then he said — and Ma murmured the translation into Jomo's ear — "My ministers wished to offer congratulations, saying this beast proves Heaven's favor. I have declined the ceremony. If the empire is well governed, there will be signs enough in the granaries. But it is," he added, in a voice that had briefly stopped being an emperor's, "a most excellent animal. It shall want for nothing."

Afterward, court painters painted Twiga's portrait on silk, with a poem about auspicious signs, while she ate the ornamental plum branches. She was given a courtyard, a keeper's staff — and, at Jomo's stubborn insistence through interpreters, a standing order for acacia, or the nearest the imperial gardens could grow.

Ma found Jomo at the rail of the returning fleet, some months later, watching the coast of Africa rise.

"Well? You saw the Middle Kingdom. What will you tell Malindi?"

Jomo thought about it: the wooden cities, the fish-needle pointing south, the emperor who knew a giraffe when he saw one and kept the wonder anyway.

"That the world is enormous," he said at last, "and that everyone in it is hungry for marvels. And that the marvel was never Twiga." He grinned. "It's that a girl giraffe from Malindi has a palace in China, and the two ends of the world have eaten at the same table."

Ma considered this, nodded, and corrected his grip on the rail.

They sailed home before the monsoon, the great sails full of ordinary wind.

## What's true in this story

- Jomo, Ma's evening visits, and Twiga's name are invented — but the giraffe is real. In 1414 a giraffe from Malindi (in modern Kenya) was presented to the Yongle Emperor of Ming China, and in 1415 Malindi's own ambassadors brought a second one directly. Court officials proclaimed it a qilin — the auspicious "Chinese unicorn" said to appear under virtuous rule.
- The emperor's cool response is historical: Yongle — who had indeed taken the throne in civil war and needed good omens — nevertheless declined formal congratulations, remarking in substance that good government mattered more than auspicious beasts. A court painting of the giraffe with its keeper, by the artist Shen Du, survives in later copies.
- The treasure fleet is real and was staggering: seven voyages between 1405 and 1433 under the admiral Zheng He, with on the order of 300 ships and some 27,000 personnel on the great expeditions — including interpreters (one of them, Ma Huan, wrote a wonderful account) and roughly 180 medical officers. The biggest ships were enormous — likely 60 to 75 meters long — though old records claim even more, and historians still argue.
- The navigation is accurate: magnetized needles floated in water bowls, time kept by burning incense sticks, and latitude checked by sighting Polaris against graduated boards.
- The fleet's purpose was diplomacy, trade, and prestige along long-established Indian Ocean routes — showing the flag from Southeast Asia to Arabia and East Africa — not conquest or "discovery"; Arab, Indian, Persian, and Swahili sailors had linked these coasts for centuries. Dozens of kingdoms sent tribute embassies to the Ming court aboard the fleet, exactly like Malindi's.
- After 1433 the voyages ended as Ming priorities turned inland — a gradual policy shift, not a dramatic burning of the fleet, whatever legend says. The giraffes stayed, presumably still receiving the nearest thing to acacia the imperial gardens could grow.
