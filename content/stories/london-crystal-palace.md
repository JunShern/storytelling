## Before we begin

Tonight we're off to London, England, in the summer of 1851 — the time of Queen Victoria, when railways were brand new and half the world seemed to be being invented at once. That summer, in Hyde Park, Britain held the first world's fair: the Great Exhibition, inside an enormous building of glass and iron that everyone called the Crystal Palace. Two coins for your pocket: a *shilling* (SHILL-ing) was a silver coin — real money for a working family; and the *Koh-i-Noor* (KOH-ih-noor) was one of the most famous diamonds in the world. Our girl is Martha, from a farming village. All aboard.

## The story

The trouble with Martha's little brother Tom was that he had never once in his life stayed where he was put.

"Hold his hand in the crowd," their mother said. "Martha — are you listening?"

"Holding his hand," Martha reported, holding it.

They had saved all spring for this day — pennies into the village club, week after week — because this was the summer there was only one place in England anyone wanted to go. In London, people said, they had built a palace out of glass and put the whole world inside it. Martha half believed it and half didn't, which is the most exciting way to believe anything.

The excursion train left before dawn, breathing steam like a dragon that had decided to be helpful — one of the cheap-fare specials now running from all over the country, so that farm families and mill families could see the great show. The whole carriage sang most of the way.

And then: Hyde Park. Martha looked up and stopped walking.

It was true. All of it was true. Above the trees rose a building of glass and iron, long as a village street and taller than a church. Papa read from his penny guidebook in the voice he used for scripture: "Nearly three hundred thousand panes of glass. A third of a mile long. Designed — it says here — not by an architect but by a *gardener*. Mr. Paxton, who built glasshouses, and sketched his first idea on blotting paper in a dull meeting. He learned the trick of the great roof from a giant water lily — the ribs of its leaf so strong his little daughter could stand on it floating."

"A palace from a lily leaf," said Mama.

They paid their shillings — it was one of the cheap days, made specially so plain working people could come — and stepped inside, into falling light and the sound of a hundred thousand footsteps. Full-grown elm trees stood *inside* the building, alive and rustling, because the builders had been forbidden to cut them down and so had simply built the palace over them, sparrows and all. At the heart of everything chimed a fountain nearly thirty feet high, made entirely of pink glass.

"Meeting point," said Papa, planting his finger at it. "If we're separated, come to the crystal fountain. Everyone see it?"

They saw the machinery courts first, because Tom would have died otherwise — printing presses hammering, looms clattering, an envelope machine folding envelopes faster than a hundred clerks, all so loud Mama covered her ears while Tom expanded like bread in an oven. They queued for the famous Koh-i-Noor diamond in its golden cage, and here is the honest truth: it was disappointing. Uncut diamonds don't blaze, and the crowd shuffled past murmuring that it might as well be glass.

It happened at the eighty-bladed knife. Martha stopped to marvel at it — a knife with eighty blades, which everyone agreed was eighty too many — turned to say so to Tom, and her hand was empty.

"Tom?" The crowd flowed past like a river in Sunday best. "*TOM?*"

What Martha did next she was proud of all her life: she stood still, closed her eyes, and thought. *He's seven. He won't be frightened — he ought to be, but he won't be. Where does a boy go when his adventure runs out?* Where everyone in the whole glass world had agreed to go.

She made for the crystal fountain, dodging bonnets, heart banging like the printing hall. And there he was — sitting on the fountain's rim, swinging his boots, deep in conversation with a policeman.

"That your brother, miss?"

"Unfortunately," said Martha, and hugged him hard enough to make him squeak.

"I wasn't *lost*," Tom protested. "I went back for the envelope machine, and then I came to the fountain like Papa said. And this policeman has seen the Queen! She comes over and over, dozens of times, like an ordinary person!"

"You," said Martha, "are having your hand held until you're forty."

After the scolding and the second round of hugging, there was fizzy lemonade — no beer in the palace, only soft drinks and buns by the hundred thousand — and a penny each for the most modern marvel of all: clean washrooms with flushing water closets, a wonder in themselves to a village family. Then home on the evening train, four people among the six million visits counted that summer, as if the whole nation had agreed to come and see what the world could make.

"Where does it all go, after?" Martha asked, her head on Papa's arm, Tom asleep with his hand — she checked — firmly in hers.

"They say the profits will build museums. Great free museums, so children can keep seeing wonders forever."

Fields went by, then stars. A palace grown from a lily leaf, Martha thought, and a brother lost and found by a fountain, because in all that world under one roof, everyone had agreed on one small meeting place.

The wheels sang along the rails: *you saw it, you saw it, you saw it.*

And Martha slept all the way home.

## What's true in this story

- Martha's family is invented, but families exactly like theirs went: villages really saved up in clubs, and cheap excursion trains — including trainloads organized by Thomas Cook — brought ordinary people to London in history's first wave of mass tourism.
- The Crystal Palace was real: about 1,848 feet long, nearly 293,000 panes of glass, erected in Hyde Park in well under a year, and genuinely designed by Joseph Paxton, a gardener — blotting-paper sketch, water-lily inspiration, standing daughter and all.
- The arched roof really was built to spare Hyde Park's elms, which lived inside the building, sparrows included; and the pink glass Crystal Fountain really was the agreed meeting point for separated visitors — which is how Martha finds Tom.
- The Exhibition ran 1 May – 15 October 1851, with about 100,000 exhibits, half from outside Britain. The deafening machinery courts, the envelope machine, and the eighty-bladed knife were all really there — and the Koh-i-Noor really disappointed the crowds, who could scarcely believe it wasn't glass. (Grown-up readers deserve the fuller truth: the diamond had recently been taken by the British Empire from a maharaja who was only a boy, and it remains contested today.)
- One shilling was the cheap-day price — about 4.5 million of the roughly 6 million admissions were shilling-day visits. Queen Victoria really visited more than thirty times; Schweppes sold over a million bottles of soft drinks; and 827,280 visitors paid a penny for the pioneering public flushing toilets, a likely origin of "spending a penny."
- The profits — about £186,000 — really did buy the land where the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum, and the Natural History Museum stand today, free to curious children ever since. The palace itself burned down in 1936, so today it can only be visited in stories.
