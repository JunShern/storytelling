## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from the United States, about one hundred and seventy years ago — a time when slavery was still legal in the American South, and enslaved people risked everything to escape north to freedom. The secret network of helpers and hiding places that guided them was called the *Underground Railroad* — though it was not underground, and not a railroad: its "stations" were safe houses, and its "conductors" were guides. The greatest conductor of all was Harriet Tubman, who had escaped slavery herself and kept going back. The people she led whispered another name for her: *Moses*. This story has some worry in it, but hold on — it ends safe.

## The story

Ben was woken by his mother's hand and a whisper: "It's tonight."

It was a Saturday night in winter, and that was no accident. Ben's mother had explained it in a voice softer than breathing: notices about runaways couldn't be printed in the newspapers until Monday. Leave on Saturday, and you bought yourself a whole day's head start before the hunters even knew your name. And winter — winter meant long nights, and nobody idling outdoors to see you pass.

At the edge of the woods stood a small woman in a dark coat. Ben had expected Moses to be a giant. She came up to his mother's shoulder.

"Eyes to me," she said quietly. "I'm Harriet. We walk at night. We sleep hid by day. You do exactly what I say, when I say it." She looked at Ben, not unkindly. "Can you be quiet, even scared?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then we're halfway there already."

They walked. Frozen mud, pine woods, the river always somewhere on their left like a dark companion. When Ben's legs shook, Harriet knelt beside him and pointed up through the branches.

"See the drinking gourd?" she said. Seven stars, shaped like a cup with a long handle — the one people up north called the Big Dipper. "The two stars at the front of the cup — follow them, and they point you to that little steady star. That's the North Star. It doesn't wander and it doesn't lie. All this walking, we're only doing what that star says."

Once, lantern light swung on a far road, and Harriet pressed them flat in a ditch, her hand steady on Ben's back until the dark went quiet again. Once she made a sound exactly like an owl, and an owl answered — except it wasn't an owl, it was a friend, and a barn door opened for them, and there was bread and a warm loft and a man who never asked their names.

"Why do these folks help?" Ben whispered. "It's against the law."

"Some laws are wrong," his mother said. "These are people who know it."

There were days in attics and one long afternoon in a potato hole, breathing the smell of cold earth while wagon wheels passed above. There was a ride hidden under sacks that smelled of onions. And there was the night Ben's fear finally spilled over and he began to cry that he couldn't, he couldn't walk anymore, they should go back —

Harriet took his face in her two hands.

"Listen to me. I've carried people to freedom for years, and I mean to say at the end of it: I never ran my train off the track, and I never lost a passenger. You are my passenger. I don't lose passengers. Now stand up."

He stood up.

In Wilmington, a white-haired Quaker gentleman named Thomas Garrett met them as calmly as if they were expected guests — which they were; he had helped so many freedom-seekers that he'd lost count — and gave Ben new shoes for his ruined ones. In Philadelphia, a Black gentleman named Mr. Still wrote their names, carefully, in a great book, so that scattered families might one day find each other in its pages.

"Are we free now?" Ben asked.

"Almost," Harriet said. "There's a new law lets hunters chase you even here. So we go on — all the way north, to Canada, where no law can touch you. One more ride. This one," and something like a smile crossed her face, "on a real railroad."

The train ran north through the winter fields, and Ben slept against his mother, properly, deeply, for the first time in three weeks.

He woke in Canada. Snow on the rooftops of a town called St. Catharines, smoke rising from chimneys of free people. His mother was looking out the window with tears shining and her chin high.

That night, in a warm room, Ben looked out at the sky before bed. The drinking gourd hung over the rooftops, and the North Star sat steady in its place — done guiding him, but still there, the way true things are.

Somewhere south, he knew, Harriet was already turning back — there were more passengers waiting.

Ben slept, free.

## What's true in this story

- Ben and his mother are invented, but their journey is drawn from real ones. Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849, then returned about thirteen times over the next decade, personally guiding some seventy people to freedom and instructing dozens more — mostly family and friends from Maryland's Eastern Shore. (Older books say "19 trips, 300 people"; historians have corrected the numbers.)
- Her methods in the story are her real ones: leaving on Saturday nights so runaway notices couldn't appear until Monday, traveling in winter nights, navigating by the North Star, imitating bird calls as signals, and hiding people in attics, barns, and potato holes.
- The "drinking gourd" really was folk language for the Big Dipper, whose front stars point to the North Star. (The famous song about it wasn't written down until much later, so the story keeps to the stars themselves.)
- Thomas Garrett, the Quaker stationmaster of Wilmington who helped some 2,700 freedom-seekers, and William Still of Philadelphia, who recorded arrivals in a great book so families could find each other, were real — and both really worked with Tubman.
- The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 really did allow escapees to be hunted even in the free North, so Tubman led her passengers on to Canada — she herself lived for years in St. Catharines, Ontario.
- Her words in the story are close to words recorded from her: "I never ran my train off the track, and I never lost a passenger." She couldn't read or write, so all her quotes come to us through others — but that claim, everyone agreed, was true.
- She later served the Union Army in the Civil War as a scout — guiding a raid that freed over 700 people in a single night — and lived to 1913, campaigning for women's right to vote.
