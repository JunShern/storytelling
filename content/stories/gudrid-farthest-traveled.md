## Before we begin

Tonight's story is a thousand years old, and it comes from the world of the Vikings — the Norse people of Scandinavia, who sailed farther than anyone in their age. It's told the way they told stories: by a *saga*-teller, at a fireside, from memory. Three names: our heroine is Gudrid (GOO-drid), a real Norse woman; her husband is Thorfinn (THOR-fin); and *Vinland* (VIN-land) is the Norse name for a green coast west across the ocean — the edge of North America, five hundred years before Columbus. The fire is lit. The teller begins.

## The story

Come closer, and I will tell you of the most traveled woman in the world.

The sagas say — and you must always begin so, for all we know of her comes down the years by firelight, polished and repolished like sea-stone — the sagas say Gudrid was born in Iceland, and was fair, and wise, and knew how to be at home anywhere, which is the rarest kind of wisdom.

Iceland, you must know, was not the edge of the world, though it looks it. West of it lay Greenland, where the Norse had two little settlements clinging to the fjords. And west of *that* — sailors blown off course had glimpsed it — lay coasts no one had walked: forests, meadows, a land with no name.

At the fires of Greenland, Gudrid heard the talk. Leif Erikson had gone and come back rich with timber and tales. And one winter a trader from Norway sat down beside her — Thorfinn Karlsefni, whose name means *the makings of a man* — and the two of them married, and hatched between them a plan bigger than any before it.

"Not a raid," said Thorfinn, at the assembly. "Not a look-and-back. A *settlement*. We take ship-loads of us — men and women both, cattle, sheep, tools, everything. We go to Vinland and we *live*."

"And who sails as its lady?" someone asked.

"I do," said Gudrid, in the voice of a woman answering an easy question.

The sagas say they went in several ships, scores of people — some say sixty, some say a hundred and sixty; sagas count the way grandfathers count fish. They took livestock lowing in the holds, and one bad-tempered bull, remember the bull. Their ship was a knarr — no dragon-headed war-longship, but the deep-bellied trading ship, the ocean-crosser, the pickup wagon of the northern seas. And no, before you ask: no horned helmets. Not on any Viking, ever. That is a story younger than your grandmother.

Land rose at last — and what a land. Grass richer than Greenland's dreams. Rivers so thick with fish you could scorn the small ones. Timber past counting — and timber, children, to a Greenlander is what gold is to a king, for in all Greenland hardly a decent tree grows. They raised turf houses by a bay, and set the smithy smoking, and there, in the first winter or the second, Gudrid bore a son and called him Snorri.

Mark that name. Snorri, son of Gudrid: the first child of European kin ever born in that whole vast land across the ocean. The sagas say he was a fine baby. All sagas say all babies are fine babies. In this case I choose to believe them.

But the land was not empty. It was home already — to people the Norse called Skraelings, an ugly word, a stranger's word for strangers; their own names for themselves the sagas never troubled to learn, which tells you something about the Norse, none of it flattering. At first there was trading, wary and wonderful: furs from the people of the land, red cloth and milk from the Norse, neither side speaking a word the other knew, both sides watching hands and eyes.

And then — the sagas say the bull got loose, bellowing like the end of the world, and terror led to anger, and anger to fighting, and there were deaths on both sides. I will not sweeten it: the newcomers had come uninvited, and the land's own people fought for what was theirs, and who can wonder? Remember, too, that we hear only the Norse side of it — the saga is one voice, and the other voices were never written down.

Around the third winter, Thorfinn and Gudrid looked at it squarely: a handful of settlers, an ocean from help, in a land whose people — rightly — did not want them. "A rich land," the saga has them say, "but never a peaceful one for us."

So they sailed home, with Snorri the Vinland-born chattering at the rail. Not a conquest. A very Norse ending: a hard venture, honestly tried, wisely walked away from — carrying timber, furs, and the best story in the northern world.

And Gudrid's road ran on. She farmed in Iceland; and when her hair was gray, the sagas say, she walked — *walked*, the breadth of Europe — on pilgrimage to Rome, and came home to end her days a holy woman, an anchoress by her son's church. From Vinland in the west to Rome in the south: no woman of her age, and few men, ever saw so much of the world. Her own people called her *vidforla* — the far-traveled.

Is it true, you ask? All of it? Ah. For a thousand years, you could shrug. But listen: in 1960, searchers found turf-house ruins at the northern tip of Newfoundland — Norse ruins, with a smithy, and a spindle whorl for spinning thread, which is to say: *women were there*. Scientists have now dated wood the Norse cut there to the exact year 1021. The sagas were polishing a true stone all along.

So when you cross the sea one day, remember you follow a woman with a baby on her hip, a thousand years ahead of you.

Sleep now. The fire's low, and Gudrid would tell you: rest well tonight; the world is wide tomorrow.

## What's true in this story

- Gudrid Thorbjarnardottir, Thorfinn Karlsefni, and baby Snorri are real people of the sagas. Nearly everything about them comes from two medieval Icelandic sagas written down generations after the events — which is why our teller keeps saying "the sagas say." The sagas even disagree with each other (60 vs. 160 settlers, for instance), just as the teller admits.
- The Vinland voyage around 1000–1010 CE is genuine history: Norse Greenlanders, following Leif Erikson's earlier voyage, attempted a settlement on the coast of North America — with livestock, women as well as men, and trade goods. Snorri is the first European known to have been born in the Americas.
- The archaeology is real and spectacular: at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, in 1960, Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad found Norse turf buildings with an iron smithy and a soapstone spindle whorl — proof that Norse women were present. In 2021, scientists dated worked wood from the site to exactly 1021 CE, using a cosmic-ray spike in tree rings. Butternuts found there grow only farther south — the Norse explored well beyond the camp.
- The encounter with Indigenous peoples happened: the sagas describe trading furs for cloth and milk, then deadly conflict — including a panicking bull. The story flags what the sagas won't: "Skraeling" was a Norse slur, the accounts are one-sided, and the Norse were the intruders. The settlement was abandoned after a few winters.
- The knarr cargo ship is accurate — and Vikings really never wore horned helmets; that's a 19th-century costume invention.
- Gudrid's later pilgrimage to Rome and her byname "vidforla" (far-traveled) are saga tradition — unverifiable, but it's how her own culture chose to remember her: as the woman who went farthest.
- All of this happened nearly five centuries before Columbus sailed — the Norse reached America first among Europeans, and Gudrid's family tried to live there.
