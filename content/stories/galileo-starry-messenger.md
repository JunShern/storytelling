## Before we begin

Tonight's story comes from Padua (PAD-you-ah), a university town in northern Italy, in the winter of 1610 — four hundred years ago, when nearly everyone believed the whole sky turned around the Earth. Our storyteller is Galileo (gal-ih-LAY-oh), a mathematics professor who has built himself a *telescope* (TEL-eh-scope) — a new invention, a tube with two glass lenses that makes far things look near. And one bright name to watch for: Jupiter (JOO-pih-ter), the largest planet, shining in the winter sky like a steady lamp. Now — open the diary, and dress warmly. We'll be on the roof.

## The story

*Padua, January 1610. Cold beyond argument. Notes of what I have seen — and I must write plainly, for no one has seen it before.*

**January 7, evening.** Jupiter stands bright in the east. I turned the new instrument upon it — my best yet; I have learned to grind the lenses myself, and this one makes things twenty times nearer. My neighbors think me mad on the roof each night. My daughter Virginia, who is nine and merciless, asks whether I have found her a new moon yet. Child, the Moon is taken. It has mountains — I measured their heights from their shadows last month, whatever the philosophers say about perfect heavenly spheres.

Tonight, a small oddity, which I note dutifully: beside Jupiter stand three little stars, quite bright, all three in a dead straight line through the planet. Two east of it, one west. Pretty. Probably nothing. The sky is full of small stars; my tube finds hundreds that no eye ever saw — the Milky Way itself, I have found, is not a cloud but stars past counting, like spilled grain.

**January 8.** I looked again — from habit, I confess, not expectation. And I sat up so fast I struck my head on the tile. The three little stars are all *west* of Jupiter tonight. All three. In a line still.

Stars do not walk. Jupiter wanders — it is a planet, that is what "planet" means, wanderer — so perhaps Jupiter has moved past them eastward? But the astronomers' tables say Jupiter moves *westward* these weeks. Either the tables err, or... something. I must look tomorrow. I dislike mysteries at bedtime; they follow me under the blanket.

**January 9.** Clouds. I stood on the roof and despised them personally. My daughter informs me one cannot despise weather. I informed her that with sufficient scholarship, one can.

**January 10.** Clear. Only two of the little stars now — both east — and the third nowhere. Hidden behind Jupiter, I think. And here is the thought I hardly dare write: the little stars are not being left behind by Jupiter. *They are keeping up with it.* Jupiter drifts against the true stars night by night — and these three drift *with* it, as gulls keep pace with a ship.

**January 11–12.** Two, then three again, rearranged. They swing east and west of the planet, never far, like lanterns on an invisible string. I have begun to measure their separations each night, and my hand shakes — from cold, I insist.

**January 13.** A fourth. Four little stars, and now there is no escape from it. They are not stars. They are *moons* — four moons — and they are circling Jupiter as our Moon circles the Earth. I have watched a thing no human eye has seen since the world began: another center of turning, hung in the sky.

Let me say it carefully, as it must be said to the philosophers, who will not enjoy it. We are taught that everything — Sun, Moon, planets, stars — circles the Earth, the one center of all motion. But here are four bodies that circle *Jupiter*. Whatever else is true, there is more than one center in this universe. The old scheme is not merely dented. It has a hole in it the size of a planet.

I went down and woke the household and made them climb to the roof, and my daughter looked long at Jupiter and its little court and said: "They are like a mother duck with her ducklings." And I, who intend to name them for the grand Medici family of Florence — for a professor must eat, and princes love a gift of moons — privately concede that hers is the better name.

**January 15.** I am writing it all into a little book, fast as the printer can set type: the Moon's mountains, the uncountable stars, the four new moons, with my diagrams night by night — dots and circles, Jupiter and its ducklings, dancing across the pages. I shall call it *Sidereus Nuncius* — the Starry Messenger. Everything in it a plain report: I looked, on these dates; I saw this; here are my drawings; look for yourself.

That last is the whole of my method, if anyone asks. *Look for yourself.* The old books are the work of great men, but the sky is the work of God, and it is still up there, publishing nightly.

**March, added later.** The book is printed — five hundred copies, sold out, I am told, within the week. Letters arrive from everywhere; the great Kepler in Prague writes his approval. Some philosophers refuse to look through the tube at all, saying that if the moons are not in the old books, they cannot be in the sky. I have offered them the roof, the chair, and the warm blanket. The moons, I find, do not require their permission.

Tonight I showed Virginia once more before her bedtime prayers. The four were all west of the planet, in a little slanted line, like children walking home.

"Will they still be there when I'm old?" she asked.

They will circle Jupiter, I told her, long after everyone who refused to look is forgotten. Now sleep — and mind the mysteries; they follow you under the blanket.

## What's true in this story

- The dates, positions, and discoveries are Galileo's real observation record, as published in his *Sidereus Nuncius*: three "fixed stars" in a line by Jupiter on 7 January 1610, all mysteriously west on 8 January, clouds on the 9th, and the crucial fourth companion on 13 January — leading to his conclusion that they were moons orbiting Jupiter. His actual nightly diagrams appear in the book.
- The supporting discoveries are real too: in late 1609 he observed the Moon's cratered, mountainous surface (measuring mountain heights from their shadows) and resolved the Milky Way into countless individual stars. He didn't invent the telescope — he heard of the Dutch device in 1609 and rapidly improved it, grinding his own lenses to about 20x power.
- The stakes are drawn honestly: the moons proved there was more than one center of motion in the heavens — a genuine blow to the Earth-centered scheme — though not yet proof that the Earth moves. And in 1610 Galileo was celebrated, not persecuted; his troubles with the Inquisition came two decades later, over other books.
- He really named the moons the "Medicean Stars" for the Medici of Florence, and it worked — he was made the Grand Duke's philosopher and mathematician. Posterity overruled him: we call them the Galilean moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto).
- *Sidereus Nuncius* was printed in Venice in March 1610 — about 550 copies, quickly sold out — and Kepler endorsed the discoveries within weeks. Some philosophers really did refuse to look through the telescope.
- Virginia was real: Galileo's eldest daughter, nine years old in 1610. Her duckling remark is invented — but she grew up to be his beloved correspondent, and over a hundred of her letters to him survive.
- A confession for accuracy's sake: telescope views of that era were dim, narrow, and wobbly — Galileo's genius was partly sheer patience, returning night after night to squint, measure, and draw. Which is, of course, the story's point: look for yourself, and keep looking.
