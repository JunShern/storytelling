## Before we begin

Tonight's story is set in England, in 1666 — three and a half centuries ago, when a terrible plague was sweeping through London and the universities had sent their students home. Our story follows one of them: Isaac Newton (NOO-tun), twenty-three years old, back at his mother's farmhouse at Woolsthorpe (WOOL-sthorp), in the quiet English countryside. Two words to know: *gravity* (GRAV-ih-tee) — the pull that draws falling things toward the Earth; and a *prism* (PRIZ-um) — a triangular piece of glass that turns a beam of sunlight into a band of colors. This story happens mostly inside one unusual head.

## The story

The young man measuring the afternoon light in his mother's parlor was, by most practical measures, a disappointing farmer.

Sent out to mind the sheep, he read. Sent to market, he paid a servant to do the selling and read. His mother, Hannah, had long since concluded — with the particular sigh of a mother who is secretly not sorry — that the farm would have to survive without him and Cambridge could have him. And now Cambridge had sent him back: the plague was in the cities, killing by the thousands, and the university had closed its gates and scattered its scholars to the countryside until the danger passed.

So here he was. Isaac Newton, twenty-three, home at Woolsthorpe with nothing — nothing! — to do.

*Nothing to do.* Inside his head, where we are permitted to sit tonight, the phrase made him almost laugh. He had never had so much to do in his life.

Understand what it was like in there. Questions did not visit Isaac's mind; they moved in and paid no rent. What is light made of? Why does the Moon not fly away? What is the mathematics of a thing that is always changing — a speed in the very act of speeding up? At Cambridge there had been lectures and errands and other people. Here there was bread, ink, silence, and the enormous sky over the fields. His mother fed him. He forgot the meals half the time; his notebooks did not.

He had bought a glass prism at the fair — a toy, sold to children to make pretty colors. He darkened his room, bored a small hole in the window shutter, and let one clean blade of sunlight cross the room to the far wall: twenty-two feet. Through the prism it went — and unfolded into a painted band, red to violet, far longer than it had any right to be.

*Everyone says the glass stains the light,* he thought, kneeling by the wall like a man at church. *Adds the color, the way a dyer stains cloth. But then why is the band so long? What if the colors are not added at all? What if they were always there — white light not pure, but a braid — and the glass merely... unbraids it?*

He would spend years teasing that thread. But the thought itself, arriving in a farmhouse bedroom while plague emptied the cities: *sunlight is a braid of every color* — he wrote it into his notebook in his tiny, patient handwriting, one question quietly interrogated onto paper, and no one in the world knew.

That was the strangest part, which he noticed sometimes at supper: *no one knew.* His mother asked if he was well. The servants thought him idle. And in his room, in stacks of paper, something was assembling itself — a new mathematics, worked out that autumn, that could hold motion still long enough to measure it; the anatomy of light; and the question that came to him in the garden.

The garden. An orchard, really — his mother's apple trees, one old cooking-apple tree in particular. He sat beneath it on fine evenings when his head was too full, which was the only kind of rest he took, and one evening an apple dropped. Not on his head — the head is a later decoration, added by people who like their stories to go *bonk* — just an apple, falling, as apples had fallen in front of every human being since the beginning of human beings.

*Why,* thought Isaac — and here the whole world tilts — *does it always fall* down? *Not sideways. Not upward. Always toward the Earth's center, as if drawn. Some pull reaches up through the branches and takes hold of it. How high does that pull reach? Above the treetops, surely. Above the clouds? Above the mountains — above the air itself? What if it reaches...* 

He looked up, over the orchard, where the early Moon was rising, pale as a held breath.

*...all the way?*

What if the Moon is falling too — always falling around the Earth, the way a thrown stone falls in a curve, but flying so fast sideways that it forever misses? One pull. The same pull. The apple and the Moon obeying one law, written across the whole of creation in mathematics. He went inside and began to calculate — rough figures, the Moon's distance, the strength of the pull dwindling with distance — and the numbers came out *near*. Not perfect. Pretty nearly, he would say, decades on. Near enough to keep.

He told no one. He published nothing — not for twenty years would the world hear it whole. He was secretive, and young, and busy, and the plague passed, and Cambridge reopened, and the young man rode back to university with saddlebags full of the future, looking exactly like a farmer's son who had wasted two quiet years.

An old man long afterward — famous now, the most celebrated mind in Europe — he sat in a London garden drinking tea with a friend, and told him the story himself: how the notion of gravity came into his mind on just such an evening, occasioned by the fall of an apple. And of those plague years he wrote: *I was in the prime of my age for invention, and minded mathematics and philosophy more than at any time since.*

The tree, they say, still stands at Woolsthorpe — gnarled, propped, and stubborn. Apples still drop in the autumn, straight down, every single time.

Now they fall for you too. Notice one, someday, and ask it a question.

## What's true in this story

- The setting is real: Cambridge closed during the great plague of 1665–66 (which killed roughly 100,000 in London), and Newton, then 22–23, spent most of about eighteen months at Woolsthorpe Manor, his mother Hannah's farm — with one brief return to Cambridge in between. He really had been a famously hopeless farmhand who read when he should have been marketing.
- The "year of wonders" is his own account: in a memorandum written in old age, Newton said that in 1665–66 he developed his "method of fluxions" (the beginnings of calculus), his theory of colors, and the idea of gravity extending to the Moon — finding the calculation answer "pretty nearly." Historians add a caution the story respects: he wrote that summary some fifty years later, smoothing years of later work into one golden season. The plague years were the seedbed, not the finished harvest.
- The prism experiments are real — a prism bought at Stourbridge Fair, a darkened room, a small hole in the shutter, a spectrum thrown some 22 feet — though he refined the work at Cambridge afterward, and exactly which experiments happened at Woolsthorpe is uncertain, so the story keeps it to first thoughts.
- The apple is genuine — as a falling apple, not a head-bonk. Newton himself, aged over 80, told the story to his friend William Stukeley in a London garden: the idea of gravity came "occasioned by the fall of an apple, as he sat in contemplative mood," wondering why apples always fall toward the Earth's center. The bonk was added by later embellishers.
- The tree at Woolsthorpe — a cooking variety called Flower of Kent — still stands, centuries old, propped and flourishing, one of the most visited trees in England.
- His secrecy is accurate: tiny handwriting, crammed notebooks, forgotten meals, and nothing published for years — the world's greatest physics lived in a drawer until the *Principia* finally appeared in 1687.
