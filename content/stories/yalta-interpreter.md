## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from February 1945 — eighty years ago, in the last winter of the Second World War. In a palace by the Black Sea, at Yalta (YAWL-tah), in Crimea, the leaders of the three great Allied powers met to plan the war's end: Winston Churchill (CHUR-chill) of Britain, Franklin Roosevelt (ROSE-uh-velt) of America, and Joseph Stalin (STAH-lin) of the Soviet Union — history calls them the Big Three. But none of them spoke the others' languages. Between them sat three nearly invisible men: the *interpreters*, who turned every sentence from English to Russian and back. Our story sits in their chairs.

## The story

The most powerful room in the world had a round table, three old men, and three younger men nobody photographed.

Arthur Birse — Major Birse, Churchill's interpreter, a banker in ordinary life, born in Russia to a British family so that both languages lived in him like two hands — took his seat just behind Churchill's shoulder and checked his pencils. Across the table he nodded to his opposite numbers: Bohlen, the American, lean and quick, who would interpret for Roosevelt while somehow also scribbling the official record; and Pavlov, the Russian, not yet thirty, who served Stalin and never seemed to sleep.

The plenary session began, and the strange music started — the music only interpreters hear from inside.

Churchill spoke the way a river floods: magnificent, rolling, clause upon clause, quotation upon flourish. Then he paused — and Birse, pencil flying in his own private shorthand, had to make it happen again in Russian: the same force, the same warning wrapped in courtesy, while the room waited and thirty men watched his mouth. Then Stalin, in Russian: quiet, brief, exact — he rarely wasted a word, which interpreters privately appreciate — and Pavlov's English came out clipped and careful. Then Roosevelt, gracious, charming the table like a host at dinner — and Bohlen rendering it into Russian with one hand while the other wrote down what everyone had said for the archives of the United States.

They were deciding, sentence by relayed sentence, the shape of the coming world: what would become of defeated Germany, divided into zones; the founding of a new organization of nations to keep the peace, to meet in San Francisco that spring; the future of Poland, argued day after day after day; the war still raging in the Pacific. Every word of it — every promise, every carefully vague phrase — passed through three unphotographed men with pencils.

"My hand," Birse confided to Bohlen in a corridor, "has taken more of this conference than my mind has."

"Your hand is writing history," said Bohlen. "My hand is writing history twice. Once as translation, once as minutes."

"And Pavlov?"

They looked down the corridor at the young Russian, gray with fatigue, being handed yet another document. Interpreters do not envy easily — they know too much about each other's work — but all three knew Pavlov had the hardest chair at the table.

The palace itself was a story: the tsar's old summer palace, white stone above the winter sea, stripped bare by the retreating German army and refurnished in ten frantic days — so that outside the grand conference room, generals slept six to a room, queued grumbling for the few bathrooms, and did battle with bedbugs; the Americans sprayed everything, twice. The drive in from the airfield had taken hours, through mountains still scattered with burnt-out tanks. This was the thing the photographs never showed, Birse thought: the war was *right there*, outside the window. Roosevelt had seen the ruins and gone quiet. He looked terribly frail that week — thin, gray, his great cape hiding how the war had used him up — though when the arguments sharpened, Birse noticed, the President's mind arrived sharp and on time. Two months later he would be dead, and the world would wonder; the interpreters, who had watched him closest, would always speak of him gently.

And then there were the dinners.

You have never seen dinners like these: courses beyond counting, caviar and sturgeon and suckling pig, Georgian wine — and toasts. Toast after toast after toast, dozens in a night, each one a small speech, each one needing interpretation — which meant that while everyone else raised glasses, three men stood working behind three chairs, stomachs growling, turning compliments into compliments.

Until the night at Stalin's own dinner when the host rose, glass in hand, and the interpreters readied their pencils for one more toast to victory or friendship — and Stalin said:

"Tonight, and on other occasions, we three leaders have got together. We talk, we eat and drink, and we enjoy ourselves. But meanwhile our three interpreters have to work, and their work is not easy. They have no time to eat or drink. We rely on them to transmit our ideas to each other. I propose a toast — to our interpreters."

And the most feared man in Europe walked around the table and clinked glasses with Birse, with Bohlen, with exhausted Pavlov — while the mighty of the earth stood and drank to the men with the pencils.

Churchill, who could no more resist a joke than a cigar, was on his feet at once: "Interpreters of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but your audience!" — and the room roared, and even Stalin laughed and laughed.

Birse wrote it all down. Of course he did. It was the job.

The conference ended with signatures and hope — real hope; the three powers had held together, the war's end was in sight, and the papers promised free elections for the peoples of liberated Europe. History must tell the rest honestly: east of the new lines, those promises were broken, and Poland — the country argued over longest at that round table — waited more than forty years for the free vote it was pledged. The interpreters lived to see all of it, and wrote their memoirs, and in them you can still hear the room: the flooding river of Churchill, the quiet exactness of Stalin, the failing, gallant Roosevelt — heard closer than anyone else on earth ever heard them, by three tired men who ate afterward, in the kitchen, comparing notes in two languages.

To the workers of the words — good night.

## What's true in this story

- The Yalta Conference met 4–11 February 1945 at the Livadia Palace, the tsars' old summer palace above the Black Sea. The three interpreters are real: Major Arthur Birse for Churchill (Russian-born, a banker in peacetime), Charles "Chip" Bohlen for Roosevelt (who genuinely interpreted and took the official American notes simultaneously), and Vladimir Pavlov for Stalin, still in his twenties.
- Stalin's toast to the interpreters is genuine, recorded in both Birse's and Bohlen's memoirs, very close to the wording here — including his walking around the table to clink glasses. Churchill's reply, "Interpreters of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but your audience!", is equally well attested, and Stalin's delight at it too.
- The working method is accurate: consecutive interpretation with rapid note-taking, no headphones or booths (simultaneous interpretation arrived at the Nuremberg trials the next year). The banquets, the dozens of toasts in a night, and interpreters working hungry through dinners are all from the memoirs.
- The setting details are true: the palace stripped by the retreating Germans and hastily refitted, overcrowded rooms, bathroom queues, DDT against bedbugs, and the hours-long mountain drive from Saki airfield past war wreckage.
- Roosevelt really was gravely ill and visibly failing — he died on 12 April 1945, two months later — but Bohlen, watching closest, insisted he remained mentally sharp at the sessions, and the story follows his eyewitness judgment.
- The agenda and the aftermath are told straight: Germany's occupation zones, the United Nations founding conference set for San Francisco, Soviet entry into the war against Japan — and the pledge of "free and unfettered elections" for Poland, which Stalin's regime broke. Yalta was hailed at the time and later became, for Eastern Europe, a byword for abandonment; historians note the Red Army already held the ground either way. The story keeps both truths, as the interpreters' own memoirs do.
