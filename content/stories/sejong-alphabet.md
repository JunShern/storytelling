## Before we begin

Tonight's story is from Korea, from the city now called Seoul (SOLE), almost six hundred years ago. Korea's king is Sejong (SAY-jong), remembered today as Sejong the Great. In his time, reading and writing meant Chinese characters — thousands of them, taking years of leisure to learn, so ordinary people, and especially ordinary women, could not read at all. One more word: *Hangul* (HAHN-gool) is the name later given to the Korean alphabet in this story. Our heroine is Sora (SOH-rah), a girl who works in the palace kitchens. Winter is coming, and something new is stirring.

## The story

Sora could not read, and mostly it didn't matter — soup doesn't need reading — but twice a year it mattered terribly. Twice a year, a letter came from her mother in the far south, and Sora had to pay a clerk to read it aloud, and stand there while her mother's voice came out of a stranger's mouth. And because letters home cost too — a clerk to write, coins she rarely had — her answers were short and stiff and never said anything true.

"You could learn your letters," teased Min, the errand boy, who was studying to be a clerk himself.

"Could I? How many characters must a clerk know?"

"A few thousand," Min admitted. "It takes years. If you do nothing else. Which you can't, because of the soup."

"Well then," said Sora, and that was that — until the winter morning the palace went strange.

The scholars were upset. You could tell because they walked faster than dignity allowed, robes snapping, and gathered in knots, hissing. Min came skidding into the kitchen with the news.

"The king! You know he's been shut away for months — everyone thought he was ill. He's been *making letters*. New ones. An alphabet — he announced it to the court! Twenty-eight letters, and they say — Sora, they say it's for *everyone*. For farmers. For women." He caught his breath. "Some of the great scholars are furious. One has written a protest to the king's face — says the classics live in the Chinese characters, that we'll become barbarians, that—" he dropped his voice and made his face long and grave, "—*learning will fall into ruin.*"

"Because of twenty-eight letters?"

"Because of who might use them," said the old cook, without turning from her pot. It was the most political sentence anyone had ever heard her say.

Sora thought about it all day, scrubbing. Twenty-eight. She could count to twenty-eight in onions.

The proclamation, when Min got hold of a copy and had it read to him so he could carry it in his head, said this — and Sora made him say the king's part twice: *Our language differs from Chinese, so the poor people, though they have something to say, cannot express their feelings. Saddened by this, I have made twenty-eight letters, that everyone may easily learn them and use them daily.*

*Saddened by this.* Kings were not, in Sora's experience, sad about her.

"There's more," Min said. "One of the king's own scholars writes that the wise can learn the whole alphabet in a morning, and even a slow fellow in ten days. And the letters are *pictures*, Sora — of your own mouth! The sound *g* is drawn like your tongue's root against your throat. The sound *m* is a little square — your two lips. You write what your mouth does."

"Show me."

He drew ㄱ in the flour dust: one bent stroke. "Tongue." Then ㅁ: a square. "Lips." Then the vowels: a dot for heaven, a flat stroke for earth, a standing stroke for a human being. "Every vowel is built from those three. Sky, ground, person. That's all."

Sora looked at the marks in the flour for a long moment. Then she said, "Make me the sounds of my mother's name."

It took him a while, being new at it himself. But it took *a while* — not years. And there in the flour dust of the palace kitchen stood her mother's name, which Sora had never once seen with her own eyes, drawn in shapes her own mouth made. She did not cry, because the soup was at a critical stage. She came close, though.

Ten days later — the slow-fellow's allotment, which she found funny — Sora could read every public notice on the palace walls, sounding them out under her breath like a woman praying. That spring, she paid no clerk. She bought paper instead, borrowed Min's brush, and wrote to her mother in the new letters — a real letter, a true one, about chilblains and the head cook's temper and a bird she'd seen on the frozen pond, in her own hand, in her own words, the first of her family in all the generations back to the beginning of the world to write anything to anyone.

Her mother couldn't read it, of course. Not yet. But the letter ended: *Find a child who knows the new letters to read this to you. Then make them teach you. It takes ten days. The king says so.*

The scholars went on arguing for four hundred years, and for four hundred years the grand documents stayed in Chinese, and the grand men called the new script vulgar — kitchen letters, women's letters. And for four hundred years, in kitchens and back rooms, in letters between mothers and daughters, in stories copied and passed hand to hand, the women's letters quietly won. Today the alphabet Sejong made has a name — Hangul — and a national holiday, and scholars around the world call it perhaps the most scientific script ever designed, and every child in Korea learns it faster than their parents can believe.

*The poor people, though they have something to say, cannot express their feelings.* Sora had something to say. It took ten days and twenty-eight letters, and after that, no one ever again stood between her voice and her mother.

Sleep well — and if you can read this, thank a teacher, and a sad king.

## What's true in this story

- Sora and Min are invented; everything around them is real. King Sejong announced the new alphabet in 1443 and promulgated it in 1446 in a document called *Hunminjeongeum* — "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people." Court records credit the invention to Sejong personally, likely working with a small group of scholars.
- The king's words in the story are his real preface, lightly condensed: our language differs from Chinese, the common people "though they have something to say, cannot express their feelings," and — "saddened by this" — he made twenty-eight letters for everyone to learn easily and use daily.
- The "morning / ten days" line is real too, from the postface by the scholar Jeong Inji (the story is careful to put it in a scholar's mouth, not the king's): a wise man can learn the letters before the morning is over, and even a slow man within ten days.
- The design really works as described: consonants are drawn as diagrams of the mouth making them (ㄱ the tongue root at the throat, ㅁ the lips), and vowels are built from three philosophical elements — heaven (a dot), earth (a flat line), human (a standing line). Linguists genuinely rank Hangul among the most rationally designed scripts in the world.
- The backlash is historical: the scholar Choe Manri and colleagues submitted a formal protest in 1444, fearing abandonment of Chinese characters would sever Korea from civilization and learning — a real intellectual anxiety of the age, not cartoon villainy.
- The long afterlife is true: elite documents kept to Chinese for centuries while the alphabet thrived in women's letters, popular fiction, and daily life, sometimes dismissed as "women's script." It became Korea's official script in 1894. The name "Hangul" was coined in the early 20th century; South Korea celebrates Hangul Day every October 9, and the original *Hunminjeongeum* manuscript, rediscovered in 1940, is on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
