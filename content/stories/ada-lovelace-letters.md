## Before we begin

Tonight's story is set in London in 1843, in the age of steam engines and gaslight — told in letters, because that is how its two heroes really talked. Ada Lovelace (AY-duh LUV-liss) was a countess and a mathematician, the daughter of the famous poet Lord Byron. Her friend Charles Babbage (BAB-ij) was an inventor who had designed — but never built — a room-sized thinking machine he called the Analytical Engine. One more word: the *Jacquard loom* (zhah-KARD) was a weaving machine that read its patterns from cards punched with holes. Remember those holes. Now — the post has arrived.

## The story

*Ockham Park, June 1843*

Dear Mr. Babbage,

I have been at my desk since breakfast, and my candle is burning low, and I am more convinced than ever that the world does not understand what you have designed. They think your Analytical Engine is a great brass calculator — a machine for sums. They are wrong, and I intend to say so in print.

Here is what I see. Your engine reads its instructions from punched cards, like the Jacquard looms that weave silk flowers in Lyon. But a loom does not know it is making flowers. It only follows the holes. And so I shall write it plainly: *the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves.*

And if patterns — why only numbers? Anything that can be written in symbols and rules, your engine could weave. Give it the rules of harmony, and it *might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music*. Do I go too far?

Yours, A.A.L.

---

*Dorset Street, London*

My dear Lady Lovelace,

You go exactly far enough, which no one else in England has managed. Twenty years I have begged Parliament for funds, and they see only an expensive abacus. You have seen the point of the thing better than the men who mocked it.

As to your translation of the Italian paper on my engine — your own notes have now grown three times longer than the paper itself, and considerably better. Press on.

C. Babbage

---

Dear Mr. Babbage,

Press on! He says press on, as if the printer were not waiting, my table of Bernoulli's numbers half-inked, and the baby with a cough besides.

Very well. I enclose Note G. In it I have set out, step by step, operation by operation, exactly how the engine would compute those numbers — every card, every turn, in order, including the steps that repeat in a loop, like a stitch worked over and over. It is a strange feeling, sir. I have written instructions for a machine that does not exist, in a language no machine has ever read. It runs perfectly — but only inside my head.

Check my arithmetic. If an error stands in print, I shall be cross with both of us.

In haste, A.A.L.

---

My dear Lady Lovelace,

I have checked it. It is right — righter than my own drafts of the same, which I shall not mention loudly. When the Notes are published, sign them as you please; those who matter will learn soon enough who "A.A.L." is.

Forget this world and all its troubles and its multitudinous charlatans — everything, in short, but the Enchantress of Number.

Your faithful servant, C. Babbage

---

The Notes were published that September, signed only *A.A.L.* The Analytical Engine was never built — not in Babbage's lifetime, not in Ada's, which was far too short. The great brass mill never turned; her program never ran.

And yet.

A little over a century later, when people finally built machines that read instructions and wove patterns of symbols — machines we call computers — they went looking for their ancestors, and found a countess's Notes from 1843. There it all was, waiting: the loops, the cards, the patient steps. The idea that a machine might handle music and words and pictures, not just sums. A program, written for a computer that didn't exist, by a woman who saw it anyway.

Somewhere in London that autumn, Ada snuffed her candle, well pleased with Note G, and went to bed — a hundred years early, and exactly on time.

## What's true in this story

- The letters here are invented in wording, but Ada Lovelace and Charles Babbage really did correspond constantly through 1843 — sometimes several letters a day — as she raced to finish her famous Notes, juggling deadlines, illness, and young children.
- The italicized lines are her real published words: the Analytical Engine "weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves," and it "might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music." And Babbage really did write to her, on 9 September 1843, about forgetting everything "but the Enchantress of Number."
- The Analytical Engine was a real design — with a "mill" (processor), a "store" (memory), and punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard silk looms — and it was never built. Ada's Note G, a step-by-step table for computing Bernoulli numbers, complete with repeating loops, is widely called the first published computer program. (It even contained a small error — the first bug.)
- Historians note the work was a true collaboration — Babbage supplied mathematics and had sketched instruction sequences before — but the extended vision in the Notes, that a computing machine could manipulate any symbols, music included, was Ada's.
- She published as "A.A.L." and died just nine years later, at 36. Modern programmers named a computer language "Ada" in her honor.
