## Before we begin

Tonight's story happened only a generation ago, in Berlin, the capital of Germany — within living memory; perhaps someone you love remembers watching it on television. After the Second World War, Berlin was split in two: West and East, capitalism and communism. In 1961 the East built a concrete wall through the middle of the city — through streets, through families — and for twenty-eight years, East Berliners could not cross it. Guards were ordered to stop anyone who tried; at least one hundred and forty people died at that Wall. One name to know: Bornholmer Straße (BORN-hol-mer SHTRAH-seh), a border crossing on an ordinary street. Tonight is November 9th, 1989.

## The story

On the east side of the Wall, in a flat that smelled of supper, a grandmother named Margarete was watching the evening news, which was how the whole thing started — with television, and a man reading a piece of paper badly.

On the west side, twenty minutes' walk and an entire world away, her grandson Thomas was doing his homework. He had never met her, not properly. He knew her as a voice on rationed phone calls and a hand waving from a window on the other side, the year they'd all stood on viewing platforms like relatives at an aquarium. His mother had been west visiting when the Wall went up in '61, a girl of nineteen; her mother had been home in the east. A city cut in half on a Sunday morning, and their family with it, for twenty-eight years.

All autumn, something had been shifting. Margarete felt it in her stomach, half hope, half dread. Her neighbors had fled west through Hungary, where the border fence was suddenly full of holes. In Leipzig, every Monday, thousands upon thousands marched with candles, chanting three words: *Wir sind das Volk.* We are the people. The government reshuffled itself nervously, promised new rules, and tonight one of its officials sat before the cameras reading a decree he had plainly never seen before — something about travel, permits, border crossings. He shuffled his pages. A reporter called out: *When does it take effect?*

The official peered at his paper, hesitated, and improvised the most consequential mumble of the century:

"As far as I know... immediately. Without delay."

Margarete set down her tea. That is not what those words mean, she thought. Those words never mean tonight. There will be offices, and queues, and stamps, and refusals; there always are.

But the western TV stations — everyone in the East watched western TV, it was the worst-kept secret in the country — were saying it plainly, saying it *now*: the Wall is open. And Margarete stood in her kitchen, seventy-one years old, in her house slippers, and heard doors opening all down her building, footsteps on the stairs, voices in the street — and she put on her good coat over her nightdress, took her identity card, and went out into the cold to see.

Bornholmer Straße was filling like a basin under a burst pipe. Hundreds, then thousands, breath steaming under the lamps, pressing toward the crossing. They chanted at the checkpoint, not angry so much as *enormous*: "Tor auf! Tor auf!" — Open the gate! The border guards stood behind their barriers, telephoning and telephoning, and Margarete, wedged somewhere in the middle of history, found herself thinking of the guards almost kindly: nobody had told them anything either. Their superiors, it later turned out, weren't answering — no one anywhere would give an order, and the crowd grew by the minute, and the men at the gate were left alone with twenty-eight years of orders in one hand and twenty thousand neighbors in the other.

At half past eleven, the officer in charge of the crossing — a lieutenant colonel who had served at that gate his whole career — looked at the crush of his own people and made the only decision left that didn't end in catastrophe. Not a hero's decision, he said afterward; a human one. He ordered the barrier raised.

And the East poured through, weeping and laughing, on foot and in sputtering little Trabant cars, two-stroke engines popping, into the arms of a West Berlin that came running to meet them with sparkling wine and chocolate and flowers, strangers kissing strangers, and everyone saying the same useless perfect word: *Wahnsinn* — madness, unbelievable, madness.

Thomas had run out with half his street. Somewhere around midnight, at the crossing, in the crowd — this part he would tell for the rest of his life — his mother beside him suddenly cried out and pushed forward, and an old woman in a good coat over a nightdress came through the checkpoint lights, and his mother, fifty years old, folded into her mother's arms like a girl of nineteen.

"Mutti. Mutti. Twenty-eight years—"

"Yes," said Margarete, patting her back, already being a grandmother about it. Then she held out her hand to the tall boy hanging back shyly. "And you are Thomas. I know your face from the pictures. I have known your face always." She looked around her: at the young people who had climbed on top of the Wall itself — dancing, on the Wall, at the Brandenburg Gate, where standing had meant death — at the champagne and the weeping guards and the little cars honking through.

"Come," she said. "Show me your street. I have twenty-eight years of walking to do."

No shot was fired that night. In the days after, Berliners went at the Wall with hammers and chisels — *Mauerspechte*, wall-woodpeckers, chipping the monster into souvenirs; and East families lined up for the hundred West-marks of welcome money and spent it slowly, dazed, on oranges and toys; and within a year there was no East and West Germany at all, only Germany. The Wall had stood for twenty-eight years. It fell in one evening — pushed over gently, by candles, by crowds, by a bureaucrat's fumble, and by a hundred thousand ordinary decisions to put on a coat and go outside.

Margarete walked her grandson's street that night until two in the morning. It was, she reported, an entirely ordinary street. That was the point. That had always been the point.

Sleep well — behind no wall at all.

## What's true in this story

- Margarete, Thomas, and their family are invented — but every family detail has real counterparts: the Wall went up overnight on 13 August 1961, trapping relatives on opposite sides for 28 years, with visits rare and phone lines rationed. West Berliners really did wave to relatives from viewing platforms. At least 140 people died at the Berlin Wall; the story does not soften that, and neither should we.
- The bungled press conference is real: on the evening of 9 November 1989, Politburo member Günter Schabowski read out a new travel regulation he hadn't been briefed on, and when the reporter asked when it took effect, answered "immediately, without delay" ("sofort, unverzüglich"). It was actually meant to begin the next day, with orderly permits. Western broadcasts then declared the Wall open, and the crowds made it true.
- Bornholmer Straße happened as told: thousands chanting "Tor auf!", guards phoning superiors who gave no usable orders, and at about 11:30 p.m. Lieutenant Colonel Harald Jäger — the real officer in charge — opened the barrier on his own authority to prevent a catastrophe. Some 20,000 people crossed there that night, and no shots were fired anywhere in Berlin.
- The scenes are documented: people dancing on the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate, sparkling wine and flowers pressed on strangers, cries of "Wahnsinn!", the popping Trabant cars, the "Mauerspechte" chipping souvenirs in the days after, and the DM 100 "welcome money" for eastern visitors.
- The autumn's real preludes are in the story: Hungary opening its border, the Leipzig Monday demonstrations — 70,000 people on 9 October alone, chanting "Wir sind das Volk" (We are the people).
- Germany reunified on 3 October 1990, less than eleven months later. The Wall did not literally "fall" that night — the gates opened; the demolition came later — but ask anyone who was there: it fell.
