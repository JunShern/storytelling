## Before we begin

Tonight we're going to Timbuktu (tim-buk-TOO) — a real town that still exists, at the southern edge of the Sahara desert in the West African country of Mali. Our story happens about seven hundred years ago, when Mali was one of the richest empires on Earth. Some words for the journey: the *Sahara* (suh-HAR-ah) is the world's largest hot desert — crossing it by camel took two months. The emperor of Mali was called the *Mansa* (MAHN-sah); the famous one in our story is Mansa Musa (MOO-sah). A *caravan* (KA-ra-van) is a long train of camels traveling together. And a *griot* (GREE-oh) is a singer whose job is to remember — all the history of the kingdom, kept safe in songs. Off we go.

## The story

The salt was running out, and Aminata knew it because of the soup.

"No salt again," her little brother announced, making a face at his bowl.

"Half a pinch," their mother corrected. "Which is not the same as none. And you'll thank me for that half-pinch if the caravan is late."

"The caravan is already late," said Grandfather from his corner, not looking up from his book. "Twelve days late."

The table went quiet. In Timbuktu, at the edge of the great desert, those were heavy words. The town sat in the luckiest spot in Africa — the Sahara to the north, and just to the south the Niger, one of the great rivers of the world, bending through green marshes full of rice and fish. Everything the desert had and everything the river had met in Timbuktu and shook hands. Gold came up the river from secret mines in the south. And salt — which every person, every goat, every pot of soup needed to live — came down from the desert on the backs of camels.

Not just any salt, either. It came from Taghaza, twenty days north by camel, a place so strange Aminata half thought Grandfather invented it: a mine in the middle of nothing where the workers cut salt out of the ground in great slabs, and built their houses from salt blocks, and roofed them with camel skins, because there was nothing else there to build with at all.

"Why is it late?" Aminata asked. "The azalai" — that was the great caravan — "is never this late."

"Sandstorms, perhaps. A dry well, perhaps." Grandfather closed his book gently. "The desert is a sea, child, and camels are its boats. Sometimes the sea is rough."

The next morning she went with her mother to the market, and the worry was everywhere, quiet as dust. The salt merchants' stalls stood nearly empty. A trader from Walata was telling anyone who'd listen: "Two slabs to a camel, each one heavy as a man — and the price already doubling —"

And then, near midday, a boy came running from the northern dunes shouting the word the whole town was waiting for.

"Azalai! Azalai!"

Everyone went. Merchants dropped their scales; the blacksmith left his fire; Aminata ran with her brother to the edge of the sand and stared north with a hundred other people. And there, wobbling out of the heat-shimmer, came — one camel.

One single camel, with one rider slumped against its neck.

The crowd's cheer died to a murmur. The rider was a young man, dusty as a ghost, and men ran to help him down and put a water-skin in his hands. Aminata wriggled between elbows to hear.

"The caravan lives," he croaked, and the whole marketplace breathed out at once. "A storm buried the track for three days. We lost the road. But our guide —" he drank again — "our guide found the way by the stars, and by the taste of the sand."

"The taste?" someone laughed, half in relief.

"Laugh if you like. He put a handful to his tongue and said, 'Two days east of the well.' And he was right. I rode ahead to tell Walata to send out water to meet the column — that's the custom, the caravan sends a rider before it like a spoken promise. They come behind me. Four hundred camels. You'll see their fires tonight."

Aminata's mother squeezed her shoulder so hard it almost hurt.

That evening, the whole town seemed to lean north. And Grandfather took Aminata up to the roof to watch, and while they waited he did what he always did: he taught.

"You see how one guide's knowing saved four hundred camels? Remember what is truly precious. When the Mansa went on his great pilgrimage — I was young then — he crossed this same desert to Cairo and on to Mecca, with thousands of people, and camel after camel loaded with gold. In Cairo they say he gave away so much gold that gold itself grew cheap for years — as if he'd spilled sunlight and made the sun ordinary. That's what they say, anyway; travelers do love to polish a story. The mapmakers across the sea drew him on their maps afterward, sitting on a throne, holding up a nugget like a lamp."

"All that gold," Aminata said dreamily.

"All that gold," Grandfather agreed, "and do you know what he brought *back*? Scholars. Builders. Books. He brought a poet-builder from Granada, far across the desert and the sea, and had him raise the great mosque — you've climbed it yourself at plastering time."

She had. Every year after the rains, when the mud walls had softened, the whole town climbed up together — standing on the rows of wooden beams that stuck out of the walls like a hedgehog's quills, exactly for that purpose — and slapped on fresh plaster, singing, keeping the great building alive with their own hands.

"Gold is only yellow dust," Grandfather said. "It sits in a pouch and does nothing. But a book talks to you. A book will talk to your grandchildren after I'm gone. Traders have begun carrying books across the desert to sell here, right alongside the salt — because a town where children learn is a town that buys books. Your brother with his little wooden writing-board, wiping the verses clean and writing new ones — he is the beginning of something, though he doesn't know it yet."

"And me?" Aminata asked.

"You," said Grandfather, "are the one who'll remember tonight. That is also a kind of book."

And then her brother shouted and pointed, and there they were: far out on the darkening sand, one fire, then three, then a dozen — the caravan camped for its last night out, four hundred camels kneeling under the stars, each with its two moon-pale slabs of salt. The fires looked like stars that had fallen and decided to stay.

Below in the square, the griot had already begun a new song — about a storm, and a guide who tasted the sand, and a rider who came ahead like a promise — and Aminata knew that in fifty years, children not yet born would hear it and think: I wish I had seen the fires.

"I saw the fires," she whispered to herself, to keep it.

Tomorrow there would be salt in the soup. Tonight there was singing. She fell asleep on the roof beside Grandfather before the song was done, and nobody woke her, because sleeping through a griot's song about a happy ending is a perfectly good way to hear it.

## What's true in this story

- Aminata and her family are invented, but the town, the trade, and the caravans are real. Timbuktu began around 1100 CE as a seasonal camp and by the 1300s was a growing trading town of the Mali Empire — with its great age of books and scholarship still ahead of it.
- The salt really came from Taghaza, about twenty days north by camel, where the houses were genuinely built of salt blocks roofed with camel skins — the traveler Ibn Battuta saw them himself in 1352. A camel carried two big slabs, and salt truly grew more valuable with every mile south.
- Desert caravans could number hundreds or even thousands of camels, guided by desert experts across waterless stretches; caravans really did send a rider ahead (the custom was called the *takshif*) so that water could be sent out to meet the column. Guides famously knew the desert the way sailors know the sea — stories were even told of guides who could tell their position by tasting the sand.
- Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to the 1330s, and his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 is one of history's most famous journeys. Writers in Cairo reported that he gave away so much gold that its price there fell for years — a report historians suspect grew in the telling, which is why Grandfather says so.
- The Catalan Atlas, a famous European map of 1375, really does show Mansa Musa enthroned holding a gold nugget — proof his fame crossed the Sahara and the sea.
- Musa returned with scholars and with the poet-architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili from Granada, credited with the Djinguereber mosque (traditionally dated 1327). It still stands, and Malian towns still climb the beam-ends together to re-plaster their mosques after the rains. Mud-brick building itself was a West African art long before al-Sahili arrived.
- Children really learned to read and write on wooden boards washed clean between lessons; gold dust and cowrie shells really were money; and griots — hereditary singers — really were the living memory of Mali, keeping the epic of its founder Sundiata alive to this day. Timbuktu later became so famous for books that hundreds of thousands of manuscripts survive there in family libraries.
