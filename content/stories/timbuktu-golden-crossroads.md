## Before we begin

Tonight we're going to Timbuktu (tim-buk-TOO) — a real town at the southern edge of the Sahara desert, in the West African country of Mali. Our story happens about seven hundred years ago, when Mali was one of the richest empires on Earth. Three words for the journey: a *caravan* (KA-ra-van) is a long train of camels crossing the desert together; the *Mansa* (MAHN-sah) was Mali's emperor — the famous one was Mansa Musa (MOO-sah); and a *griot* (GREE-oh) is a singer whose job is to remember the kingdom's whole history in songs. Off we go.

## The story

The salt was running out, and Aminata knew it because of the soup.

"No salt again," her little brother announced, making a face at his bowl.

"Half a pinch," their mother corrected. "And you'll thank me for it if the caravan is late."

"The caravan is already late," said Grandfather from his corner. "Twelve days."

The table went quiet. Timbuktu sat in the luckiest spot in Africa — the Sahara to the north, and just south the great river Niger, winding through marshes full of rice and fish. Everything the desert had and everything the river had met in town and shook hands. Gold came up the river from secret mines in the south. And salt — which every person, every goat, every pot of soup needed to live — came down from the desert on camels, from a place called Taghaza, twenty days north, so strange that Aminata half believed Grandfather invented it: a mine in the middle of nothing, where the workers cut salt from the ground in great slabs and built their very houses out of salt blocks, roofed with camel skins.

"Why is it late?" she asked.

"Sandstorms, perhaps. A dry well." Grandfather closed his book. "The desert is a sea, child, and camels are its boats. Sometimes the sea is rough."

Next morning the worry was all through the market, quiet as dust — until near midday a boy came running from the northern dunes shouting the word the whole town was waiting for.

"Azalai! The caravan!"

Everyone ran to the edge of the sand. Out of the shimmer came — one camel. One single camel, with one dusty rider slumped against its neck. Men helped him down and put a water-skin in his hands, and Aminata wriggled between elbows to hear.

"The caravan lives," he croaked, and the marketplace breathed out at once. "A storm buried the track for three days. But our guide found the way — by the stars, and by the taste of the sand."

"The taste?" someone laughed, giddy with relief.

"Laugh if you like. He put a handful to his tongue and said, 'Two days east of the well.' And he was right. I rode ahead so water can be sent out to meet the column — that's the custom, a rider before the caravan like a spoken promise. Four hundred camels come behind me. You'll see their fires tonight."

That evening Grandfather took Aminata up to the roof to watch, and while they waited, he taught — as he always did.

"One guide's knowing saved four hundred camels. Remember what is truly precious. When the Mansa made his great pilgrimage to Mecca — I was young — he crossed this desert with thousands of people and camel after camel loaded with gold. In Cairo they say he gave away so much that gold itself grew cheap for years — as if he'd spilled sunlight and made the sun ordinary. So they say; travelers love to polish a story. Mapmakers across the sea drew him on their maps afterward, holding up a nugget like a lamp."

"All that gold," Aminata said dreamily.

"And do you know what he brought *back*? Scholars. Builders — the great mosque you climb at plastering time, when the whole town stands on the beam-ends and slaps on fresh mud, singing. And books. Gold is only yellow dust, child; it sits in a pouch and does nothing. A book talks to you — and to your grandchildren after you're gone. Traders now carry books across the desert to sell here, right beside the salt. Because a town where children learn is a town that buys books."

Then her brother shouted and pointed: far out on the darkening sand, one fire, then three, then a dozen — the caravan camped for its last night, four hundred camels kneeling under the stars, each with its two moon-pale slabs. Fallen stars that had decided to stay.

Below in the square, the griot was already singing a new song — about a storm, a guide who tasted the sand, and a rider who came ahead like a promise. In fifty years, Aminata thought, children not yet born would hear it and wish they had seen the fires.

"I saw the fires," she whispered, to keep it.

She fell asleep on the roof before the song was done, and nobody woke her — because sleeping through a griot's song about a happy ending is a perfectly good way to hear it.

## What's true in this story

- Aminata and her family are invented, but the town, the trade, and the caravans are real. Timbuktu by the 1300s was a growing trading town of the Mali Empire, with its great age of books still ahead of it.
- The salt really came from Taghaza, about twenty days north by camel, where houses were genuinely built of salt blocks roofed with camel skins — the traveler Ibn Battuta saw them in 1352. A camel carried two big slabs, and salt truly grew more valuable with every mile south.
- Caravans could number hundreds or thousands of camels, and really did send a rider ahead (the custom was called the *takshif*) so water could be sent out to meet the column. Guides were famed for knowing the desert like a sea — stories were even told of guides who judged their position by tasting the sand.
- Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to the 1330s; his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 is one of history's most famous journeys, and Cairo writers reported he gave away so much gold its price fell for years — likely exaggerated, which is why Grandfather hedges.
- The Catalan Atlas of 1375 really shows Mansa Musa enthroned with a gold nugget, and he really brought back scholars and the architect credited with the Djinguereber mosque (1327) — which still stands, re-plastered by hand after the rains to this day.
- Griots — hereditary singers — really were the living memory of Mali, and Timbuktu later became so famous for books that hundreds of thousands of manuscripts survive there in family libraries.
