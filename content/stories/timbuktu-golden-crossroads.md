At the edge of the great desert, where the sand finally gives up and lets the grass begin, there was a town called Timbuktu. And in that town, long ago, lived a girl named Aminata.

Timbuktu was a young town then, and not a large one, but it sat in the luckiest spot in all of Africa. To the north lay the Sahara, a sea of sand that took two months to cross. To the south, a short ride away, flowed the Niger, one of the great rivers of the world, bending through green marshes full of fish and rice. Everything the desert had, and everything the river had, met in Aminata's town and shook hands.

Aminata's mother traded cloth in the market, and so Aminata knew the sounds of the town by heart: the *thump, thump* of millet being pounded in wooden mortars, the call to prayer floating from the mud-brick minaret, the creak and grumble of camels kneeling down to be unloaded. But the sound she loved best came only a few times a year, always in the cool season. It began as a shout from the boys watching the northern dunes, and it ran through the streets like water:

"Azalai! The caravan is coming!"

Then the whole town went up to watch. Out of the shimmering north came the camels — a hundred, three hundred, sometimes more, walking in one slow line that took an hour to arrive. Each camel carried two great slabs on its sides, pale as moonlight and heavy as a grown man.

Salt.

"Why salt?" Aminata had asked her mother once. "It looks like nothing. Like dirty ice."

"Because, little one, there is no salt here, and no one can live without it. People need it, goats need it, food is nothing without it. And so it comes from Taghaza, twenty days into the desert, where there is nothing *but* salt — the miners there cut it out of the ground in slabs, and they even build their houses from blocks of it. Imagine: walls of salt, roofed with camel skins. And with every step a camel takes toward the south, the salt grows more precious."

"And what goes back north?"

Her mother smiled and lowered her voice, the way everyone in Timbuktu did when they spoke of it. "Gold."

The gold came up the river from secret places in the south — forests where the miners kept their diggings hidden and asked no questions. In the market, Aminata had seen it weighed: a merchant tapping golden dust from a hollow reed onto the pan of a little scale, against polished weights, while everyone pretended not to stare. Gold dust bought salt; salt bought cloth; cloth bought fish and rice and honey; and small things — a handful of nuts, a bundle of firewood — were paid for with cowrie shells, tiny white shells with toothy smiles, carried from an ocean so far away that no one Aminata knew had ever seen it.

In those days everyone still talked about the emperor's great journey, though it had happened before Aminata could remember. The emperor of Mali — Mansa Musa, lord of the whole river and half the desert — had made the pilgrimage to Mecca, far to the east. He had crossed the desert with thousands of people and camel after camel loaded with gold, and in the great city of Cairo he had given away so much of it, to the poor and to everyone he met, that people said gold itself became less precious there for years afterward — as if he had spilled sunlight and made the sun ordinary. Traders coming back swore the whole world now knew Mali's name; years later, mapmakers across the sea would draw the emperor on their maps, seated on a throne, holding up a nugget of gold.

But Aminata's grandfather always said the emperor brought home something better than the fame.

"Books," he said. "And builders, and teachers. Gold is only yellow dust, child. It sits in a pouch and does nothing. But a book —" he touched the leather satchel where he kept his own, "a book talks to you. It talks to your grandchildren after you're gone."

Grandfather had a friend who had watched the new mosque being built — Djinguereber, the Friday mosque, raised from the earth itself: mud and rice-husk plaster over wooden beams, the ends of the beams left poking out of the walls in rows, like the quills of a hedgehog. Those beams were not a decoration. Every year, after the rains had licked the walls soft, the whole town climbed the walls together, standing on the beam-ends, slapping on fresh plaster, laughing and singing while the mosque was made new. A building of earth, kept alive by everyone's hands.

Aminata's little brother went to school in the shade beside the mosque. His whole classroom fit under one arm: a wooden board, washed clean each time, on which the teacher wrote verses in ink for the children to copy and learn. When the board was full and the lesson was learned, he washed the words away and began again. Grandfather said that some of those children would grow up to be scholars, and that traders had begun to bring books across the desert to sell here — books resting on camelback beside the salt — because a town where children learn is a town that buys books.

In the evening, when the heat let go of the day, Aminata carried water and listened to the griot singing in the square. The griot was the town's living library: he kept all the old stories in his head — the lion prince Sundiata, who founded Mali; the kings and the rains and the famines — and passed them, voice to voice, down the years.

The stars came out, enormous over the desert. Somewhere to the north, the salt caravans were camped on the cold sand, their fires like a scatter of fallen stars. Somewhere to the south, the river ran on in the dark, carrying canoes full of rice. And between them lay Timbuktu, the meeting place, the place where things changed hands: salt into gold, words into ink, stories into children.

Aminata lay down on her mat and heard, very far away, a camel complaining about something, the way camels always do.

Tomorrow there would be salt to see, and cloth to sell, and stories.

She was asleep before the griot finished his song.

## What's true in this story

- Aminata and her family are invented, but the town, the trade, and the caravans are real. Timbuktu began around 1100 CE as a seasonal camp and by the 1300s was a growing trading town of the Mali Empire — modest in size then, with its great age of scholarship still ahead of it.
- The salt really came from Taghaza, about twenty days north by camel, where the houses were genuinely built of salt blocks roofed with camel skins. The traveler Ibn Battuta saw them himself in 1352. A camel carried two big slabs, and salt truly grew more valuable with every mile south — though the old saying that it traded "for its weight in gold" is an exaggeration.
- Gold from secret mining regions in the south, salt from the north, cowrie shells as small change, and gold dust weighed on scales — all real. West Africa supplied a huge share of the entire Old World's gold in the Middle Ages.
- Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to the 1330s, and his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 is one of history's most famous journeys. Writers in Cairo reported that he gave away so much gold that its price there fell for years — a report historians think was probably exaggerated, which is why the story says "people said."
- The Catalan Atlas, a famous European map made in 1375, really does show Mansa Musa enthroned with a gold nugget — proof his fame crossed the Sahara and the sea.
- Mansa Musa returned with scholars and with the poet-architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili from Granada, who is credited with the Djinguereber mosque, traditionally dated 1327. It still stands today, and towns in Mali still climb the beam-ends together to re-plaster their mosques after the rains. (Mud-brick building itself was a West African tradition long before al-Sahili arrived.)
- Children really learned to read and write using wooden boards washed clean between lessons, and griots — hereditary singers and storytellers — really were the living memory of Mali, keeping the epic of Sundiata alive to this day. Timbuktu later became so famous for books that hundreds of thousands of manuscripts survive there in family libraries.
