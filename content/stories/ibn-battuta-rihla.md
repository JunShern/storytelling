## Before we begin

Tonight's story is set in Fez (FEZ), a city of scholars in Morocco, in North Africa, almost seven hundred years ago. An old traveler named Ibn Battuta (ib-un bat-TOO-tah) has come home after nearly thirty years on the road, and the Sultan has ordered his journey written down. The book will be called the *Rihla* (RIH-lah) — "The Travels." Two more words: the *hajj* (HAJ) is the pilgrimage to the holy city of Mecca that Muslims hope to make once in their lives; and a *qadi* (KAH-dee) is a judge. Our narrator is the unlucky young scribe holding the pen.

## The story

The Sultan of Fez himself commanded it, so I could hardly refuse: *Sit with the old traveler. Write down everything he says.*

Everything, it turned out, was a great deal.

"I left Tangier at twenty-one," Ibn Battuta began, on the first morning, settling onto his cushion like a man with vast territories to administer. "Alone, on a donkey, weeping — I missed my mother before the city walls were out of sight. I meant only to make the hajj to Mecca. Sixteen months, perhaps, there and back." He smiled into his beard. "I was gone twenty-nine years."

I wrote it down. That is what I do: I write down what he says, in the finest prose I can manage, and I do not say what I think.

What I think is that no man alive has done what this man claims.

Understand my position. Day after day he pours out marvels, and I must write them all as fact. He claims he stood in Mecca not once but many times; that he served eight years as a qadi — a judge! — in Delhi, in India, for a sultan famous across the world for showering gold on strangers one day and terrors the next; that he survived that court, which he assures me few did. He claims shipwreck off the coast of India — the ships carrying the gifts he was to bring to China, sunk before his eyes while he watched from the shore, having stayed behind to pray. He claims he judged in the Maldive Islands, where — he says — the money is seashells. Seashells! He claims China: cities without end, paper money — *paper*, used as silver! He claims he watched men climb into baskets pulled by ropes into the sky. He claims to have crossed the Sahara to the golden kingdom of Mali and back.

Seashells. Paper silver. I wrote it all, and at night I complained to my teacher at the madrasa.

"He contradicts no one," I burst out, "because no one else has been where he claims to have been! How convenient for him!"

My teacher, who is old and unfair, only stroked his beard. "You know what is said at court. Even the wazir answered the doubters: beware of calling a thing false only because your own eyes are small. And tell me — when he speaks of the places you *can* check — Cairo, Damascus, Mecca — is he accurate?"

I had to admit it. On every city I could verify, the old man was exact: this gate, that teacher, this street of coppersmiths, that misbehaving fountain.

"Then write, boy. Let God and the geographers sort him out."

So I wrote. And a strange thing happened, somewhere in the months of writing: I stopped fighting him, because I began to see the shape of the thing. This was no boaster's list. It was a world — one world, wider than any map in Fez, and stitched together, every mile of it, by faith and hospitality.

For here is what his stories are actually made of, when you write them all down: lodgings and meals. In every city from Tangier to — well, to wherever he truly reached — there were colleges for scholars, lodges for travelers, kitchens endowed by the pious so that a stranger could eat. A man of learning, he said, could cross the world owning nothing but his reputation and never sleep unsheltered. "I arrived in each place a stranger," he told me, "and left owing money to no one and dinners to everyone. Generosity is a road, boy. It runs farther than any road of stones."

And once — only once — I caught the price of it all. He had been telling of his father's death, learned years too late, in a foreign city, from a passing traveler; his mother, too, gone before his return, of the great plague, when it lately swept the world. He fell silent, and the great catalog of marvels stopped, and he was simply an old man in a courtyard in the evening, who had traded thirty years of his family for the world.

"Was it worth it?" I asked — as a person, not a pen.

He thought a long while, which I respected. "Travel," he said at last, "leaves you speechless — and then turns you into a storyteller. I could not have one gift without the other. Write that."

I wrote that.

We finished the book in two years. The scholars of Fez received it politely; some, behind their hands, still call him the greatest liar in Morocco. It occurs to me that the truth about my old man will simply have to wait — a century, perhaps several — until other travelers walk his roads and send back word.

But I have made my private peace. Last week he described the harbor of Alexandria to a merchant who knows it stone by stone, and the merchant wept at the exactness. A man does not invent with that particular tenderness. He remembers.

Twenty-nine years. Some say seventy-five thousand miles. One donkey, at the start, and a boy who missed his mother.

Write it all down, the Sultan said.

I did. And I believe it — very nearly all of it — which, for a scribe, is a kind of love.

## What's true in this story

- Ibn Battuta is real, and so is the skeptical reception. He left Tangier in 1325, aged 21, intending the pilgrimage to Mecca — and kept going for about 29 years, through Egypt, Arabia, East Africa, India, the Maldives, Southeast Asia, and (he says) China, returning via Mali. Modern estimates put his travels around 117,000 km (some 73,000 miles) — farther than any known traveler before the steam age.
- The scribe is invented in personality but not in existence: in 1354 the Sultan of Fez, Abu Inan, ordered the journey written down, and a young scholar named Ibn Juzayy spent about two years turning Ibn Battuta's dictation into the *Rihla*. Ibn Battuta's own notes had been lost in his travels — the book came from memory.
- The doubters are historical: the great historian Ibn Khaldun records that courtiers whispered Ibn Battuta was lying, and that the wazir cautioned against dismissing marvels merely because one hasn't seen them oneself. Modern scholars trust the *Rihla* broadly but genuinely doubt parts — especially whether he reached China himself — which the story lets the scribe carry.
- The "unbelievable" details are the believable ones: cowrie shells really were money in the Maldives (where he really served as judge), paper money really circulated in China, and he really was a qadi for about eight years in Delhi under the brilliant, terrifying Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq — and really watched the China-bound mission's ships wreck off Calicut.
- The world that carried him is the story's true subject: across the lands of Islam, madrasas, traveler's lodges, and pious endowments genuinely fed and housed traveling scholars, and Arabic served as a passport of the educated from Morocco to the Indian Ocean. His parents really both died during his absence — his mother in the Black Death.
- The *Rihla* was little known for centuries and nearly forgotten, until 19th-century scholars found manuscripts in Algeria — since then, travelers and historians have walked his roads and found him, again and again, exact. The closing line he dictates — travel "leaves you speechless, then turns you into a storyteller" — is the famous saying attributed to him; fittingly for this story, its attribution can't be traced past the tellers.
