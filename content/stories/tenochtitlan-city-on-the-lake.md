## Before we begin

Tonight's story is set where Mexico City stands today — but five hundred years ago, before any Europeans had arrived, when it was an island city on a lake, and one of the biggest cities in the entire world. Its name was Tenochtitlan (teh-notch-TEE-tlan), and its people called themselves the Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah) — that's where the name "Mexico" comes from. Three more words to carry with you: a *chinampa* (chee-NAHM-pah) is a garden island the farmers built by hand out in the shallow lake; *cacao* (kuh-KOW) beans are the beans chocolate is made from — and in this city, they were used as money; and our heroine's name is Xochitl (SHO-cheel), which means "flower." One thing to know: the year before our story, the lake flooded the city. Now — here we go.

## The story

"It won't grow back," Xochitl said.

She stood at the edge of the family chinampa with mud to her ankles, glaring at the ruined half of the garden as if glaring could farm.

"It will," said her father.

"The flood drowned everything on this end. The maize, the beans, grandmother's marigolds—"

"And the garden is still here," her father said. "The water took our crop, little flower. It could not take the land, because we built the land." He stamped once, and the black earth held firm under his foot. "Now. Are you helping me rebuild it, or are you decorating it?"

That made her laugh, even angry. And she took the digging stick he held out.

They were garden farmers on the southern edge of Tenochtitlan, where the city's white houses and canals dissolved into a green puzzle of island fields. Everyone called the chinampas "floating gardens," and every farmer's child learned to roll their eyes at it.

"If they floated," her father liked to say, "anyone who says so has never built one. We *make* land, we don't sail it."

And they did make it — Xochitl had helped since she was small. You fenced a rectangle of shallow lake with posts. You piled in water-weeds and rich black mud scooped from the bottom, armful by armful, layer on layer, until an island stood just above the water. Around the edge you planted willow trees, and their roots gripped everything in place like a hand holding a full plate. The lake watered your garden. The mud fed it. Nothing was wasted; even the sweepings from the city's streets came out by canoe to feed the fields.

Last spring, all that patience had met bad luck. A new aqueduct had brought too much water rushing into the city, and the lake had risen into the streets and over the low gardens. Even the emperor could not argue with water — people whispered that a wise lord had warned him it would happen, and everyone had spent that season drying out and starting over.

"Everything in this city was made," her grandmother said that evening, patting tortillas by the fire. "Remember it. When our people came to this lake long ago, we were poor and nobody wanted us. We built an island. Then a garden. Then a city of causeways and canals and — how many people, husband?"

"More than anyone can count," said Grandfather comfortably. "Two hundred thousand? Three? More than in the greatest cities across the sea, though we don't know about those, and they don't know about us."

"So," Grandmother said, pointing her spoon at Xochitl, "a half-drowned garden is nothing. Mud into flowers. Nothing into everything. That is the whole story of the Mexica."

"But can we do it before the great market day?" Xochitl asked. "We've nothing left to sell."

Her father and grandmother traded a look.

"That," her father admitted, "is the race."

The race took twelve days. Xochitl would remember them in her muscles all her life. They dredged fresh mud and spread it dark and steaming over the drowned end. They mended the willow fence. And most important of all, they went to the seed-nursery corner of the garden, where seedlings grew in little cubes of mud cut neat as honey-cake — one seed to each cube, raised snug while the main beds worked — and they transplanted, cube by cube, row by row, until the ruined half was striped with hopeful green.

"This is the trick of it," her father said, pressing a cube into place. "The garden never sleeps. While one crop ripens, the next is already a hand tall. Seven harvests in a year, if you're clever and the gods are kind."

"And if the lake behaves," said Xochitl.

"And that."

On the night before market day, they cut what the flood had spared and what the nursery had hurried along: bundles of amaranth greens, late squash, chilies, and armfuls upon armfuls of marigolds, orange as little suns, because the one thing Grandmother had replanted first — before even the maize — was flowers.

"People can skip a vegetable," she said. "Flowers, never. In this city we love them the way misers love gold."

They loaded the canoe in the dark. Nothing in the city rolled on wheels; no animal pulled a cart; every single thing anyone ate, wore, or built moved by canoe or on someone's back. At dawn the canals were as busy as anthills — canoes of fish, canoes of firewood, a canoe of turkeys complaining about the arrangement — and Xochitl knelt in the bow of her family's, parting the mist, prouder of that boatload of flood-garden marigolds than an emperor of his feathers.

The market at Tlatelolco swallowed them like a friendly whirlpool. On the great days, tens of thousands of people came — rows upon perfect rows, cloth here, feather-work there, obsidian blades, pottery, honey, medicines, dogs, everything in its ordered place, with inspectors walking the lanes to keep every trader honest and judges seated at the end to settle any argument on the spot.

A woman with silver in her hair stopped at their mat, lifted a marigold bundle, and looked at Xochitl's father.

"South gardens? I heard the flood sat on you for a month."

"It did," he said.

"And you have *flowers* already?"

Xochitl couldn't help herself. "We grew them in cubes," she blurted. "The seedlings wait in mud cubes, like babies in blankets, and when the beds are ready you tuck them in and they don't even notice they've moved—"

The woman laughed and bought four bundles, and paid in cacao beans, counting them into Grandmother's palm — the very beans chocolate is made from, which always struck Xochitl as an excellent kind of money, since in an emergency you could drink your savings.

They sold out before the sun was high.

Going home, they let the canoe drift a while. Fresh water ran beside them along the great aqueduct from the springs at Chapultepec — built double, two channels side by side, so that when one was being scrubbed clean the other kept the city drinking. Sweepers were washing down the streets; her grandmother swore a thousand of them tended the city every day, and Xochitl believed it, because she had never once seen the city dirty.

"Next year," Xochitl said sleepily, from the bottom of the canoe, "we should build the beds a hand higher. In case the lake forgets its manners again."

"Next year," her father agreed, "you'll help me do exactly that."

That night, on her reed mat, she heard the drum from the great temple counting out the hour, and the water lapping at the family canoe, and the willows whispering at the edge of the garden — the garden that had drowned and come back, because her family had made it once and simply made it again.

Mud into flowers. Nothing into everything.

She slept like a seedling in a cube of earth: snug, and certain of the morning.

## What's true in this story

- Xochitl and her family are invented, but their world is real. Tenochtitlan, founded on an island in Lake Texcoco around 1325, held perhaps 200,000 people or more by 1500 — bigger than the London or Paris of its day.
- The flood is real history: in 1499 the emperor Ahuitzotl's new Acuecuexco aqueduct brought so much water into the city that the lake rose and flooded it — and tradition says the lord Tzutzumatzin had warned him it would happen. The city spent the following seasons rebuilding, which is when our story is set.
- The chinampas ("floating gardens") really didn't float: they were artificial islands built from layered mud and water-plants, anchored by willow trees. The seedling nursery in cubes of mud, the constant replanting so no bed ever sat idle, and yields of many harvests a year are all well documented — chinampas were among the most productive farmland anywhere in the premodern world.
- The city really ran entirely on canoes and human porters — no wheeled transport, no draft animals. The Tlatelolco market was the largest in the Americas, with orderly rows, market inspectors, and on-site judges; Spanish eyewitnesses later claimed tens of thousands came on great days. Cacao beans really were money.
- The Chapultepec aqueduct really had twin channels so one could be cleaned while the other flowed, and the city was famously clean — swept daily by an army of workers, with waste collected by canoe and recycled into the gardens as fertilizer.
- The Mexica loved flowers passionately — marigolds were a major chinampa crop — and priests really did mark the hours of night from the temples with drums and conch trumpets. Every child in the city, boys and girls alike, was required to attend school: a genuine rarity anywhere in the world in 1500.
- One honest note for grown-up readers: the great temple at the city's heart was also a place where the Mexica offered human sacrifices to their gods — a real and solemn part of their world, tied to war and belief, though the wildest Spanish numbers are considered propaganda by modern scholars. This story keeps to the daily life that most of the city's people — farmers, traders, children — actually lived.
