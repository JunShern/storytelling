## Before we begin

Tonight's story is set where Mexico City stands today — but five hundred years ago, before any Europeans arrived, when it was an island city on a lake and one of the biggest cities in the world. Its name was Tenochtitlan (teh-notch-TEE-tlan), and its people, the Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah), gave Mexico its name. Three more words: a *chinampa* (chee-NAHM-pah) is a garden island the farmers built by hand in the shallow lake; *cacao* (kuh-KOW) beans — the beans chocolate is made from — were used as money; and our heroine is Xochitl (SHO-cheel), which means "flower." One last thing: the year before our story, the lake flooded the city. Here we go.

## The story

"It won't grow back," Xochitl said, glaring at the ruined half of the garden as if glaring could farm.

"It will," said her father. "The flood drowned our crop, little flower. It could not take the land — because we built the land." He stamped, and the black earth held firm. "Now. Are you helping me rebuild, or decorating?"

That made her laugh, even angry. She took the digging stick.

They were garden farmers on the southern edge of Tenochtitlan, where the city's white houses dissolved into a green puzzle of island fields. Everyone called the chinampas "floating gardens," and every farmer's child learned to roll their eyes at it. "If they floated," her father liked to say, "anyone who says so has never built one." And Xochitl had helped build one: you fenced a rectangle of shallow lake with posts, piled in water-weeds and rich black mud, armful by armful, until an island stood above the water, then planted willows around the edge so their roots gripped it all like a hand holding a full plate. The lake did the watering. The mud did the feeding.

Last spring, all that patience had met bad luck: a new aqueduct had rushed too much water into the city, and the lake had risen over streets and low gardens alike. People whispered that a wise lord had warned the emperor it would happen. Even emperors, it turned out, could not argue with water.

"Everything in this city was made," Grandmother said that evening, patting tortillas. "When our people came to this lake, we were poor and nobody wanted us. We built an island. Then a garden. Then a city of two hundred thousand people. So — a half-drowned garden is nothing. Mud into flowers. Nothing into everything."

"But can we do it before the great market day?" Xochitl asked. "We've nothing left to sell."

Her father and grandmother traded a look. "That," he admitted, "is the race."

The race took twelve days. They dredged fresh mud, dark and steaming, over the drowned end. They mended the willow fence. And most important, they went to the nursery corner, where seedlings grew in little cubes of mud cut neat as honey-cake — one seed to a cube, raised snug while the main beds worked — and transplanted, cube by cube, until the ruined half was striped with green.

"This is the trick of it," her father said. "The garden never sleeps. While one crop ripens, the next is already a hand tall. Seven harvests a year, if you're clever."

The night before market, they cut what the flood had spared and what the nursery had hurried: squash, chilies, amaranth greens — and armfuls of marigolds, because the first thing Grandmother had replanted, before even the maize, was flowers. "People can skip a vegetable," she said. "Flowers, never. In this city we love them the way misers love gold."

They loaded the canoe in the dark. Nothing in the city rolled on wheels and no animal pulled a cart; everything moved by canoe or on someone's back, and at dawn the canals were busy as anthills. The market at Tlatelolco swallowed them like a friendly whirlpool — tens of thousands of people on the great days, everything in perfect rows, inspectors walking the lanes to keep traders honest.

A woman with silver in her hair stopped at their mat. "South gardens? I heard the flood sat on you for a month."

"It did," said her father.

"And you have *flowers* already?"

"We grew them in cubes!" Xochitl blurted. "The seedlings wait in mud cubes like babies in blankets, and when the beds are ready you tuck them in and they never notice they've moved—"

The woman laughed and bought four bundles, counting cacao beans into Grandmother's palm — money you could, in an emergency, drink as chocolate, which Xochitl considered an excellent arrangement.

They sold out before the sun was high.

That night, on her reed mat, she heard the drum from the great temple counting the hour, the water lapping at the family canoe, and the willows whispering at the edge of the garden that had drowned and come back — because her family had made it once, and simply made it again.

Mud into flowers. Nothing into everything.

She slept like a seedling in a cube of earth: snug, and certain of the morning.

## What's true in this story

- Xochitl and her family are invented, but their world is real. Tenochtitlan, founded on a lake island around 1325, held perhaps 200,000 people or more by 1500 — bigger than the London or Paris of its day.
- The flood is real history: in 1499 the emperor Ahuitzotl's new aqueduct brought so much water into the city that the lake rose and flooded it — and tradition says a lord named Tzutzumatzin had warned him. The city rebuilt in the seasons that followed, which is when our story is set.
- Chinampas really didn't float: they were artificial islands of layered mud and water-plants anchored by willows. The seedling nursery in mud cubes and the constant replanting so no bed sat idle are well documented — chinampas were among the most productive farmland in the premodern world.
- The city really ran entirely on canoes and human porters — no wheels, no draft animals. The Tlatelolco market was the largest in the Americas, with inspectors and on-site judges, and cacao beans really were money.
- The city was famously clean — swept daily, with waste collected by canoe and recycled into the gardens — and the Mexica loved flowers passionately; marigolds were a major crop. Priests really marked the night hours from the temples with drums.
- One honest note for grown-up readers: the great temple was also a place of human sacrifice — a real and solemn part of Mexica religion and power, though the wildest Spanish numbers are considered propaganda by modern scholars. This story keeps to the daily life most of the city's people actually lived.
