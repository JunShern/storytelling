## Before we begin

Tonight we go to Alexandria (al-ex-AN-dree-ah), a great harbor city in Egypt on the Mediterranean Sea, about two thousand two hundred and fifty years ago — founded by Alexander the Great and ruled now by kings called the Ptolemies (TOL-eh-mees). Here stands the most famous library of the ancient world. Three words for the journey: a *scroll* is a book of that age — a long roll of paper made from the papyrus (pah-PIE-russ) reed; a *scribe* is a professional copier of texts; and the *Mouseion* (moo-SAY-on) is the palace of scholars attached to the Library. Our hero is an apprentice there. The fleet is coming in.

## The story

"Wake up, Zoe! The grain fleet is in from Rhodes — thirty ships at least!"

Most cities' children ran to the harbor to see sailors or monkeys or figs. Zoe ran with a satchel of blank scrolls, because she was fourteen and apprenticed to the Great Library, and in Alexandria the law was this: every ship that dropped anchor must be searched — not for smuggled gold, but for *books*.

"By order of the king!" her master Philon announced up the gangplank of the first ship, with Zoe scrambling behind. "All written works aboard to be declared for copying!"

The captain groaned the groan of a man who had docked here before. "It's two poems and a book on fish. Last time you kept my charts a month."

"And returned you beautiful copies," said Philon serenely. "The originals stay. The Library keeps what the Library finds. Copies for the owner — that's the bargain."

"That's robbery with extra steps and good handwriting."

"That," said Philon, "is Alexandria."

While they argued, Zoe did her actual job, which Philon called *listening at the edges*. Sailors always knew things. And today a Rhodian deckhand, paid in figs, leaned close and said the thing that changed her week:

"Old ship at the far quay — in from Athens. Cook's mate says there's a chest in the hold the captain won't open for anybody. Says what's in it is worth more than the cargo."

Books, thought Zoe, with the certainty of the truly bookstruck. Worth more than cargo meant books.

Getting Philon to the far quay took an hour of the finest whining in Egypt. The old captain met them with arms crossed, and when Philon gave his speech, the man surprised them: he laughed — sadly.

"Search away. But the chest goes to no one. My brother was a scholar of Athens. He died this spring. The chest is his books, going home to his daughter in Cyrene. I swore it."

Philon, who bullied cheerfully over commas, went quiet. Even the Library's law had never been for taking a dead man's books from his child. But Zoe said: "May we *see* them? Only see?"

Inside the chest, wrapped in oiled cloth, lay a scholar's life: Homer, geometry, plays. And at the bottom, one scroll so old its edges had gone to lace, its tag faded past reading. Zoe unrolled three lines and forgot how to breathe.

"Master. Master. It's the comedies of— we don't *have* this. It's on the wanted list. It's been on the wanted list since before I was born. This may be the last copy under the sky."

And there on the quay, the whole proud machine of Alexandria came down to this: Philon, hands clasped, *begging*. "Captain. If that scroll molders in Cyrene, the plays die with it — forever, you understand, the way a species dies. Let our scribes copy it. Seven days. I'll stand surety with my own salary — and my apprentice here as hostage; she eats little."

"Hey," said Zoe.

The captain looked at the scroll a long time. "My brother," he said finally, "used to say a book you don't share is only a dead tree." He put it — gently, gently — into Zoe's hands. "Seven days. And *two* copies. One for his daughter, mind. A fresh one she can read without it crumbling."

They ran it up through the streets like a rescued child, past the Mouseion where the salaried scholars argued under the colonnades — the king fed and paid them to do nothing but think, and their arguments were the city's best free theater. In the copying hall, twelve scribes dropped everything. Zoe herself was given three columns to copy, her pen scratching down the letters one by one while the original lay beside her, whispering across two hundred years.

Seven days later the captain sailed with two crisp new copies smelling of fresh papyrus and cedar oil. The lacework original stayed, labeled and shelved among its half-million neighbors — *from the ships*, the tag said, as so many tags in Alexandria said.

That evening Philon found Zoe still at her desk.

"Understand what we do here, apprentice. Kings think we collect scrolls, the way they collect gold. Wrong. Every book is a person talking — someone dead, or far away, or not yet born. We keep the conversation from going quiet. That's the whole job."

Zoe nodded, and rolled her copy tight, and blew out the lamp. Below in the dark harbor, the lighthouse burned over a hundred ships — every hold, perhaps, with a book in it nobody knew about yet.

Tomorrow they'd go looking. That was the whole job.

## What's true in this story

- Zoe, Philon, and the captain are invented, but the ship-search law is real: the ancient medical writer Galen records that ships docking at Alexandria were searched for books, which were seized and copied — the Library keeping the originals and returning copies. Scrolls acquired this way were tagged "from the ships."
- Galen also tells the story of the deposit: Alexandria borrowed Athens' precious official copies of the great tragedies against an enormous deposit of silver — then kept the originals and forfeited the deposit. (Galen wrote four centuries later, so historians treat the details with care — but they agree the Library's collecting was famously ruthless.)
- The Mouseion was real: scholars were salaried by the king, housed, fed, and set free to study — one visitor mockingly called it the "birdcage of the Muses." Its members included Euclid the geometer and Eratosthenes, the librarian who calculated the size of the Earth from the length of shadows — and got it astonishingly close.
- Books were papyrus scrolls, often six to ten meters long, stored with hanging name-tags and treated with cedar oil against insects. Reading was commonly done aloud. The famous counts of "500,000 scrolls" come from ancient boasts and can't be verified — the story keeps the number vague.
- The Library's first great catalog, the *Pinakes* of Callimachus — 120 scrolls just to list the books — was history's first serious library catalog. A "wanted list" of missing works is a small invention in its spirit.
- The Library did not burn down in one dramatic fire, whatever the movies say. It declined slowly over centuries — funding cuts, purges of scholars, war damage, neglect. Books die of quiet more often than of flame, which is rather the story's point.
- The lighthouse at the harbor mouth — the Pharos — was real too, one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world.
